## 1. Stated policies that anyone on call (or anyone at all) may roll back or kill a change without approval

### Takeaway

The clearest written policies are Google's ("rolls back first and investigates the problem second"; nobody asks why you rolled back) and Booking.com's ("Everyone in the company" must be able to stop a harmful experiment). Most other companies describe the practice (roll back before investigating; a named commander whose decision is final) without publishing an explicit rule about who is authorised, so "anyone may roll back without approval" is well documented as a norm at a handful of firms and much thinner as a written policy elsewhere.

### Inferences

- Three distinct authority models appear in the sources, and the essay should not blur them: (a) the releasing team or author rolls back its own change without asking (Google CRE post, Slack deploy commander); (b) anyone at all may stop a change they see causing harm (Booking.com experiments; Mace's "any oncall" panic buttons); (c) a single trained incident commander holds final authority and others do not freelance (PagerDuty, Google SRE ch. 14, Slack). Models (b) and (c) pull in opposite directions; mature organisations seem to use (a)/(b) for the first minutes and (c) once an incident is declared.
- The "no approval" part is consistently paired with an after-the-fact obligation: describe the problem in the rollback changelist (Google), write the postmortem (SRE books), notify the owner. None of the sources describes rollback authority without an audit trail.
- Booking.com's refusal to automate stops and Amazon's insistence on automating them (section 2) are both justified by "ownership". Managers should expect to choose, not to find a consensus.

### Gaps

- I found no first-party written policy from Stripe, Wise, Nubank, Starling, Shopify, GitHub or LinkedIn stating who may roll back without approval. One search across Stripe/Wise/Nubank/Starling returned only Stripe job listings (automated rollback listed as a skill) and a secondary newsletter; nothing citable.
- "Anyone can declare an incident" at Monzo: not confirmed in the 2019 post as extracted. Monzo's open-sourced incident tooling documentation was not read.
- Honeycomb / Charity Majors: one search found only general on-call essays, no statement on rollback authority. No claim made.
- Netflix "Full Cycle Developers", Etsy Code as Craft, ACM Queue ("Fail at Scale", Maurer 2015) and CACM all returned HTTP 403; web.archive.org is blocked for my fetch tool. "Fail at Scale" is NOT READ and nothing is claimed from it.
- No source gives a formal access-control description (for example, which roles hold the production permission to trigger rollback).

## 2. Automatic rollback as delegated authority

### Takeaway

Amazon, Slack, Monzo, Uber and Netflix describe handing the rollback decision to the deployment system, mainly because humans do not watch deployments and react too slowly; Slack reports the largest measured effect. The sources are nearly silent on false-positive cost and override policy, and Facebook's SOSP paper records an engineer overriding a correct canary rejection, so override rights are a real risk that the vendor-style success stories do not discuss.

### Cited Findings

- Clare Liguori (AWS), "Automating safe, hands-off deployments", Amazon Builders' Library, 18 June 2020. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "The deployment system actively monitors an alarm to determine if it needs to automatically roll back a deployment." Why: "Most deployments aren't actively watched by a team member" and "These deployments are completely hands-off." — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)
- Same article [CROSS-REF `amazon_netflix_linkedin_etsy.md`, full text there]: the same alarm pages on-call and triggers rollback; "Often, the rollback is already in progress by the time the oncall engineer has been paged". — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)
- Same article, override exists but for the blocking direction: "The pipeline prevents automatic deployments to production when there is a higher risk of causing a negative impact." and "These deployment blockers can be overridden by developers". Time windows define "when a deployment is allowed to start". The extraction found no discussion of false positives or of roll-forward. — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)
- Will Sewell and Joseph Pallamidessi (Monzo), "Argo Rollouts at scale: Bringing Automated Rollbacks to 2,100+ services at Monzo", Monzo blog, November 2022 (URL path dated 2 November; page reported 7 November). [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Motivation: "a bad release could be rolled out to all pods before an engineer would be alerted". Principle: "Humans notoriously miss stuff all the time; discipline is not a good alternative to automation". — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/11/02/argo-rollouts-at-scale)
- Same post, decision rule: rollbacks trigger "based on the same metrics and thresholds we use for our generic alerts", for example RPC error rate above 20 percent, any panics, large spikes in database errors. Cost: "it could now take >5 minutes for all of the analyses to run." Scale: "We have over 2,100 services". — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/11/02/argo-rollouts-at-scale)
- Same post, limits: no rollback counts, no time-to-mitigate figures, no false-positive discussion, no opt-out policy; the claim of benefit is that it "has saved us from a number of bad deployments to production." Lesson: "migration and tooling work was >90% of the effort." Monzo is a regulated UK bank, which makes this the most relevant automatic-rollback source for the essay's audience. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/11/02/argo-rollouts-at-scale)
- Sam Bailey (Senior Engineering Manager, Slack), "Deploy Safety: Reducing customer impact from change", Slack Engineering, 7 October 2025. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Goals: "Automated detection & remediation within 10 minutes" and "Manual detection & remediation within 20 minutes". — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)
- Same post, sequencing: alerts with manual remediation came first, then "What we needed was automatic instead of manual remediation." and "Once automatic rollbacks were introduced we observed dramatic improvement in results." (figures in section 7). — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)
- Same post, adoption lesson: "Folks will delay adoption of a practice until they're familiar or comfortable with it" and "Use the tooling often, not just for the infrequent worst case scenarios." The post does not discuss false positives or who may override. — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)
- Aron Lorincz, Goncalo Alvarez, Rasmus Vestergaard (Uber), "Controlling the Rollout of Large-Scale Monorepo Changes", Uber Engineering blog, 28 August 2025. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Deployment signals allow "the deployment system to automatically roll back to a safe version." When a cross-service issue is flagged, "the author of the change is immediately notified, so they can investigate." — [Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/controlling-the-rollout-of-large-scale-monorepo-changes/)
- Same post, the cost of gating: target of "a maximum of 24 hours to unblock all services"; an early simulation predicted "tier 1 services would be blocked for 40 hours." Blast-radius data: "1.4% of commits affect more than 100 services and 0.3% of commits affect more than 1,000 services." No override authority is named. — [Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/controlling-the-rollout-of-large-scale-monorepo-changes/)
- Netflix, Graff and Sanden, "Automated Canary Analysis at Netflix with Kayenta", 10 April 2018. [NOT READ by me, 403; CROSS-REF `amazon_netflix_linkedin_etsy.md` N3, full text there]: Spinnaker "makes the decision to continue, rollback, or, in some cases, prompt manual intervention to proceed." — [Netflix TechBlog](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69)
- *Site Reliability Workbook*, ch. 16 "Canarying Releases". [TOOL-EXTRACT]. On a bad canary: "we should pause and roll back the deployment, or perhaps contact a human to help troubleshoot the issue." On over-sensitive metrics: "we will get lots of false positives; that is, we will think a canary deployment is bad, even though it isn't." — [SRE Workbook ch. 16](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
- *Site Reliability Engineering*, ch. 1 "Introduction". [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Change-management best practice is automation of progressive rollouts, detection and "Rolling back changes safely when problems arise"; "By removing humans from the loop, these practices avoid the normal problems of fatigue". — [SRE book ch. 1](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)
- Counter-evidence on override. Tang et al., "Holistic Configuration Management at Facebook", SOSP 2015, §6.4. [CROSS-REF `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`, full text of §6.4 read there]: an engineer overrode a canary rejection ("It must be a false positive of the canary tool!") and caused crashes. — [Tang et al. SOSP 2015](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2015/current/2015-Monterey/printable/008-tang.pdf)
- Counter-evidence on automation itself. Microsoft Azure AD, 28 September 2020. [CROSS-REF `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`; Microsoft's RCA read there only via The Register]: a defect "corrupted the deployment metadata", automated rollback failed and rollback had to be manual. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/10/02/microsoft_azure_bug/)
- Counter-position: Booking.com chose not to automate stops although it "could easily" (section 1). [FULL-TEXT]. — [arXiv 1710.08217](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1710.08217)

### Inferences

- The stated reason for removing the human is the same everywhere: detection-to-action latency and inattention, not distrust of engineers' judgement. Amazon and Monzo both wire rollback to the same signals that page a human, which makes automatic rollback a faster execution of a decision the organisation had already decided the on-call person would make.
- Slack's sequencing (alerts, then manual rollback, then automatic) is a practical migration path for a bank: prove that the alert-to-manual-rollback loop is trustworthy before letting the pipeline pull the cord. This is my reading of the post's order of events, not a rule the post states.
- Silence on false positives is itself a finding. None of Amazon, Monzo, Slack or Uber quantifies wrongly rolled-back deployments. The Workbook warns that false positives will be numerous if metrics are poorly chosen, and the Facebook example shows the human response to a suspected false positive (override) is where harm entered.
- For payment systems, an automatic rollback is only as safe as the compatibility guarantees in section 3; delegating authority to a pipeline does not remove the precondition.

### Gaps

- No source read gives a false-positive rate for automatic rollback, nor a written override policy (who may force a deployment past a failed analysis, and with what review).
- Google's Canary Analysis Service paper (Davidovic and Beyer, ACM Queue 2018) was not read (403 recorded by another researcher). LinkedIn: no rollback-specific source found in this project.
- Facebook Gatekeeper/Configerator automatic revert: only the §6.4 examples above; I did not read text describing automatic revert mechanics.
- Monzo's ArgoCon 2022 talk exists as video; not watched, nothing claimed from it.

## 3. What must be true for rollback to be safe (and where rollback does not apply)

### Takeaway

Practitioner sources agree that rollback is safe only if adjacent versions can coexist and each deployment has been verified in both directions; the commonest breakage is a protocol or data-format change. Google and AWS both say the rollback path must be exercised routinely, and both warn that teams "think they have safe rollbacks" until an outage proves otherwise. None of the sources read addresses external side effects such as payments already sent; for those, code rollback stops further harm but does not undo anything.

### Cited Findings

- Sandeep Pokkunuri (formerly Principal Engineer, AWS), "Ensuring rollback safety during deployments", Amazon Builders' Library, 5 December 2019. [TOOL-EXTRACT by me; passages FULL-TEXT in `amazon_netflix_linkedin_etsy.md` A6]. "We found that the most common reason for not being able to roll back is a change of protocol." — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/ensuring-rollback-safety-during-deployments/)
- Same article, technique: "Therefore, we divide such a change into two parts and perform a two-phase deployment." Prepare: every server can read the new format but still writes the old. Activate: servers begin writing the new. A bake period of "usually a few days" sits between them. — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/ensuring-rollback-safety-during-deployments/)
- Same article, verification: "Therefore, we explicitly verify that each deployment is safe for rolling forward and backward." The pipeline test deploys to about half a fleet for version coexistence, completes, then rolls back, checking for errors at each step. — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/ensuring-rollback-safety-during-deployments/)
- Same article, limits stated by the author: "In this article, I assume that both versions of the software function as expected." and "our guidelines are not applicable for all applications and scenarios." It gives no statistics. It is about stored and wire formats, not external side effects. — [AWS Builders' Library](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/ensuring-rollback-safety-during-deployments/)
- Hilton, Google Cloud Blog, 2017, section "Incompatible changes". [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "The danger is that you release the new binary, upgrade the database schema, and then find a problem with the binary that necessitates rollback." Result: "a binary that doesn't expect the new schema, and hasn't been tested with it." — [Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons)
- Same source, section "Testing rollbacks": "If you haven't rolled back in a few weeks, you should do a rollback 'just because'" in order "to find any traps with incompatible versions, broken automation/testing etc." Also: "you need to plan for the situation where any one of your dependencies is suddenly rolled back by its owners." — [Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons)
- Mace 2020. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "Far too many think they have safe rollbacks, only to learn otherwise during an outage." And: "by exercising your mitigations regularly, you validate their behaviors." And: "Unfamiliar tooling is dangerous even in the hands of skilled oncallers." — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/content/generic-mitigations/)
- Mace 2020, on improvising a switch mid-incident: "Attempts to create a new degradation mechanism while your system burns will always bite you." — [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/content/generic-mitigations/)
- *Site Reliability Engineering*, ch. 13 "Emergency Response", "Test-Induced Emergency". [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "We attempted to roll back the permissions change, but were unsuccessful." Finding: "Because we hadn't tested our rollback procedures in a test environment, these procedures were flawed". Remedy: "We now require thorough testing of rollback procedures before such large-scale tests." — [SRE book ch. 13](https://sre.google/sre-book/emergency-response/)
- Same chapter, "Change-Induced Emergency" (the positive case): the engineer "rolled back the change almost immediately" and "Had this swift rollback not occurred, the outage could have lasted considerably longer". Even so services "didn't fully recover for up to an hour." — [SRE book ch. 13](https://sre.google/sre-book/emergency-response/)
- *Site Reliability Workbook*, ch. 10 "Postmortem Culture", example good postmortem action item. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "Monitoring target safety checks should not allow you to push a change that cannot be rolled back." — [SRE Workbook ch. 10](https://sre.google/workbook/postmortem-culture/)
- *Site Reliability Workbook*, ch. 9, case study 2. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Rollback was slow because of tooling: "pushing a new configuration meant rebuilding binaries, which took about an hour." The chapter concludes responders "would have benefited from some sort of tool that facilitated rollbacks." — [SRE Workbook ch. 9](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/)
- Roll-forward versus roll-back. Hilton 2017. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "The temptation for many bugs... is to build a quick patch and then 'roll forward'" (trimmed). Google's view: "We don't generally recommend this though, especially if the bug in question is user-visible". Reason: "Under this kind of pressure, coding, testing or deployment mistakes are almost inevitable." — [Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons)
- Flag-off versus code revert. Rossi 2017 (Facebook). [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "we can simply switch the gatekeeper off rather than revert back to a previous version." — [Engineering at Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)
- Flag-off can fail too. Brian Harry, "A Rough Patch", 25 November 2013. [CROSS-REF `microsoft.md` S14, full text there]: the team rolled back all the feature switches enabled that morning; "Unfortunately it didn't. And no one is completely sure why." — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- State that cannot simply be failed back. GitHub, "October 21 post-incident analysis", 30 October 2018. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. After a 43-second partition, both data centres "contained writes that were not present in the other data center", so "we were unable to fail the primary back over to the US East Coast data center safely." — [GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/2018-10-30-oct21-post-incident-analysis/)
- Same GitHub post, the explicit trade-off: "our strategy was to prioritize data integrity over site usability and time to recovery." Outcome: "24 hours and 11 minutes of service degradation." Lesson: "tighter operational controls or improved response times are insufficient safeguards". — [GitHub Blog](https://github.blog/2018-10-30-oct21-post-incident-analysis/)
- Irreversible side effects in general (SECONDARY / vendor architecture guidance, search snippet only). Microsoft's Azure Architecture Center pattern page describes a compensating transaction as a new action that offsets completed work rather than restoring prior state, and advises idempotent compensation steps. [SNIPPET]. — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/azure/architecture/patterns/compensating-transaction)

### Inferences

- A checklist the essay can defend from these sources: (1) adjacent versions coexist (mixed-fleet test); (2) every deployment is verified forward and backward in the pipeline; (3) format, protocol and schema changes are split into prepare/activate (the same idea as expand/contract), with a hold between phases; (4) rollback is exercised on a schedule, not only in emergencies; (5) the rollback tool does not depend on the system that is failing (SRE ch. 13 found troubleshooting tools sat behind crash-looping jobs); (6) on-call staff have personally used the tool; (7) rollback takes minutes, not a rebuild.
- "Flag off is the long-exercised path" is my inference, not something a source here says in those words. The supporting facts are Rossi (flag off preferred to revert) and the counter-facts are Harry 2013 (flag rollback did not restore service) and Knight (section 4: the old path was dead code that had become dangerous). The off path is only safe while it is still the path most traffic takes; once a flag has been at 100 percent for months, off is the untested path.
- For money movement the reversible unit is exposure, not history. Rolling back or flipping a flag stops new erroneous payments; payments already submitted to a scheme, ledger entries posted and messages emitted need compensating actions, reconciliation and often customer redress. I found no primary engineering source that says this for payments specifically; RBS and Knight (section 4) are the documentary evidence that state outlives the rollback.
- GitHub's choice is the cleanest primary-source statement that "fastest recovery" is not always the right objective: where data integrity is at stake, a responder with unrestricted authority to "just fail back" could have made things worse.

### Gaps

- No first-party payments or core-banking engineering source was found that discusses rollback limits for already-sent payments, idempotency keys in rollback, or expand/contract by that name. The compensating-transaction material is generic and snippet-level.
- "Roll-forward only" cultures and Facebook's mobile hotfix policy: not researched to primary text here; `google_facebook_quality_ownership.md` has cherry-pick/hotfix material (Rossi 2019 transcript) but nothing I verified on a roll-forward-only rule.
- Database migration practice (forward-only migrations) was not sourced.

## 4. Cases where rollback or the kill switch caused or worsened an incident

### Takeaway

Regulator and company documents record rollbacks and kill switches that made things worse: Knight (uninstalling good code from seven servers), RBS (an untested back-out path was "the underlying cause"), Cloudflare December 2025 (a kill switch delivered through an ungraduated global configuration path), Azure AD 2020 (rollback automation broken by the same defect), and Google's own untested rollback. The lesson each source draws is about procedure and testing of the undo path, not about withholding authority; but together they argue plainly against unrestricted, improvised rollback in systems with mixed versions or durable state.

### Cited Findings

- SEC, Order Instituting Administrative and Cease-and-Desist Proceedings, In the Matter of Knight Capital Americas LLC, Release No. 34-70694, 16 October 2013. [FULL-TEXT]. ¶27 ("Incident Response"): "Knight did not have supervisory procedures to guide its relevant personnel when significant issues developed." — [SEC order ¶27](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Same order, ¶27, the rollback: "Knight uninstalled the new RLP code from the seven servers where it had been deployed correctly." Then: "This action worsened the problem, causing additional incoming parent orders to activate the Power Peg code". — [SEC order ¶27](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Same order, ¶27: Knight "relied primarily on its technology team to attempt to identify and address the SMARS problem in a live trading environment" while the system "continued to send millions of child orders". ¶10: "Knight remained connected to the markets and continued to send orders". — [SEC order ¶10, ¶27](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Same order, scale (¶16 and ¶10): about 4 million executions in 154 stocks for more than 397 million shares "in approximately 45 minutes"; Knight "lost more than $460 million". — [SEC order](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Same order, deployment controls (¶15, ¶26): no second technician reviewed the deployment; "Knight had no written procedures that required such a review." ¶41: "a written procedure requiring a simple double-check of the deployment of the RLP code could have identified that a server had been missed". — [SEC order ¶15, ¶41](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- The lesson the SEC states is about procedures, and the undertakings require a consultant to review, among other things, "incident response protocols". The order does NOT say Knight's staff lacked authority to act, nor that rollback should require approval; it says they acted without a procedure and without stopping order flow first. A market-wide disconnect (the true kill switch) was the mitigation not taken for 45 minutes. — [SEC order](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- FCA, Final Notice to Royal Bank of Scotland plc, National Westminster Bank plc and Ulster Bank Ltd, 19 November 2014. [CROSS-REF `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`, cited paragraphs read in full there]. The team backed out a batch-scheduler upgrade on 19 June 2012; "Technology Services had only tested the consequences of backing out Version 2 to Version 1." and "This was the underlying cause of the IT Incident." (¶4.8). Back-out tests "did not use representative data in the queues". Penalty £42 million (FCA). — [FCA Final Notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- Management lesson as the FCA frames it (per the sibling notes, ¶2.11): inadequate "policies for testing software" and incident processes. Again a testing-of-the-undo-path lesson. — [FCA Final Notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- Cloudflare, "5 December 2025 outage" blog post. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Timeline (UTC): 08:47 change deployed; 08:48 full impact; 08:50 incident declared via automated alerts; 09:11 change reverted; 09:12 revert fully propagated. Impact: "approximately 28% of all HTTP traffic served by Cloudflare" for "~25 minutes total impact". — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/)
- Same post, mechanism: "This killswitch system receives information from our global configuration system". And of that system: "This system does not perform gradual rollouts, but rather propagates changes within seconds to the entire fleet". — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/)
- Same post, stated lessons: "data used for rapid threat response and general configuration needs to have the same safety and blast mitigation features." Planned: fail to a known-good state on error ("the system will log the error and default to a known-good state"), "health validation and quick rollback capabilities". — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/)
- Note on Cloudflare: detection took about 3 minutes and the revert propagated in about 1 minute, but roughly 21 minutes passed between declaration and revert (08:50 to 09:11, arithmetic from the timeline above). The post as extracted does not explain that interval. — [Cloudflare blog](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/)
- Microsoft Azure AD, 28 September 2020. [CROSS-REF `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`; via The Register's quotation of Microsoft's RCA]: the deployment-system defect also "corrupted the deployment metadata", so automated rollback failed and manual rollback was needed. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/10/02/microsoft_azure_bug/)
- Google SRE book ch. 13, "Test-Induced Emergency". [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Rollback of a permissions change failed because rollback procedures had not been tested; a different, "already tested approach" restored service within an hour. — [SRE book ch. 13](https://sre.google/sre-book/emergency-response/)
- Slack, 22 February 2022 incident. [CROSS-REF `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`]: pausing a percentage-based rollout did not recover the system; metastable failures "do not normally return to a stable state without a significant reduction in load". — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/slacks-incident-on-2-22-22/)
- Azure DevOps, November 2013. [CROSS-REF `microsoft.md`]: rolling back the day's feature switches did not restore service. — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- *Site Reliability Workbook* ch. 9, case study 1. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Halting helped ("He stopped the release of version 1.88, which had rolled out to 25% of users.") but the release was then resumed too early: "it would have been better to postpone the rollout until the root cause was fully determined". — [SRE Workbook ch. 9](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/)
- Generic mitigations are not free. *Site Reliability Workbook* ch. 9: "generic mitigations are blunt instruments and may cause other disruptions to the service." Mace: "you cannot know the knock-on effects of actually using your generic mitigation until you've used it for real." [TOOL-EXTRACT]. — [SRE Workbook ch. 9](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/); [O'Reilly](https://www.oreilly.com/content/generic-mitigations/)

### Inferences

- Common structure of the failures: the undo was itself a change, travelling a path with less testing and less staging than the original change. Knight: partial fleet, removal of code that the flag depended on. RBS: back-out tested for a different version pair. Cloudflare: kill switch via the global instant path. Azure AD: rollback metadata corrupted by the faulty deployment.
- Therefore "no approval needed" is defensible only for undo actions that are pre-built, pre-tested and routinely exercised. An improvised uninstall (Knight) or first-ever back-out (RBS) is a novel change made under the worst conditions and does not belong in the pre-authorised category.
- None of the regulator documents faults the firm for letting staff act without sign-off; they fault the absence of procedures and tested paths. This matters for the essay's audience: the regulatory record supports pre-authorised, rehearsed rollback and does not support adding an approval step.
- Knight also shows the ordering problem: the first move should have been the blunt, high-level stop (disconnect from the market) rather than component-level rollback while orders kept flowing. In payments terms: suspend the rail or the flow first, then undo code.

### Gaps

- I did not read the RBS, Azure AD, Slack 2022 or Azure DevOps 2013 primary documents myself in this pass; they are cross-references to sibling notes.
- Cloudflare's post was read through extraction only; the precise defect triggered by the kill switch (rule type, proxy version) is covered, if at all, in `counter_evidence_and_failures.md` and is not asserted here.
- No public case was found where a correct rollback was blocked or delayed by an approval requirement at a bank, which would be the mirror-image evidence the essay would want.

## 5. Financial-sector framing: kill switches, emergency changes, and whether a rollback is itself a "change"

### Takeaway

Trading regulation mandates an immediate cancel capability (MiFID II RTS 6 Article 12, quoted below) and pre-trade controls under the firm's "direct and exclusive control" (SEC Rule 15c3-5), but the texts read say nothing about who inside the firm may pull the switch. Banking IT guidance (MAS, EBA, FFIEC) requires a rollback plan and a defined emergency-change procedure with named approvers; none of the texts read says a rollback needs fresh approval, and none says it does not. Treating rollback as a pre-authorised part of the original change is consistent with the texts, but I found no regulator statement or first-person bank account confirming that reading.

### Cited Findings

**Algorithmic trading: kill functionality:**

- Commission Delegated Regulation (EU) 2017/589 of 19 July 2016 (MiFID II "RTS 6"), Article 12 "Kill functionality", paragraph 1. [TOOL-EXTRACT from EUR-Lex; segments reassembled; re-check against EUR-Lex before printing]. The firm "shall be able to cancel immediately, as an emergency measure, any or all of its unexecuted orders" ... "submitted to any or all trading venues to which the investment firm is connected ('kill functionality')." — [EUR-Lex 32017R0589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)
- Article 12(2): unexecuted orders "shall include those originating from individual traders, trading desks or, where applicable, clients." Article 12(3): the firm must be able to identify which algorithm and which trader, desk or client "is responsible for each order that has been sent to a trading venue." — [EUR-Lex 32017R0589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)
- Recital 9: "An investment firm should be able to withdraw all or some of its orders where this becomes necessary". — [EUR-Lex 32017R0589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)
- Same regulation, deployment side. Article 5(2): "A person designated by the senior management of the investment firm shall authorise the deployment or substantial update" of a trading system or algorithm. Article 8 ("Controlled deployment of algorithms") requires predefined limits on instruments, order price/value/number, strategy positions and number of venues before deployment. — [EUR-Lex 32017R0589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)
- Article 11(1): any "proposed material change to the production environment" must be "preceded by a review of that change by a person designated by senior management"; depth "proportionate to the magnitude of the proposed change." — [EUR-Lex 32017R0589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)
- What RTS 6 does NOT say (as extracted): Article 12 does not specify who within the firm may trigger the kill functionality, how fast, or whether approval is needed. It is a capability requirement. Article 8 is, in effect, a regulatory requirement for limited initial exposure of a new algorithm, which is close to a mandated ramp. — [EUR-Lex 32017R0589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589)
- SEC Rule 15c3-5 (17 CFR 240.15c3-5, Market Access Rule). [TOOL-EXTRACT from Cornell LII]. - Same rule, (d): controls must be "under the direct and exclusive control of the broker or dealer". (e): a system "for regularly reviewing the effectiveness of the risk management controls" plus annual CEO certification. The rule does not use the phrase "kill switch". — [17 CFR 240.15c3-5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/17/240.15c3-5)
- SEC Knight order, ¶40. [FULL-TEXT]. Knight "lacked reasonably designed controls and supervisory procedures to detect and prevent software malfunctions that can result from code development and deployment." This is the SEC reading 15c3-5(b) as reaching deployment practice. — [SEC order ¶40](https://www.sec.gov/files/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- FIA Principal Traders Group, statement on the Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago research paper, 5 October 2012 (INDUSTRY ASSOCIATION). [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "a manual 'kill switch' should be available to those responsible for managing a trading system's risk". Also: firms "should also review, but not solely rely on, risk controls provided by the clearing firms and exchanges." — [FIA PTG statement](https://www.fia.org/fia/articles/fia-ptg-statement-federal-reserve-bank-chicago-research-paper)
- FIA response to the CFTC concept release on risk controls for automated trading, 11 December 2013 (INDUSTRY ASSOCIATION). [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Survey result: "all responding FCMs use some form of a kill switch or other means to stop order submission when necessary." A search snippet [SNIPPET] puts the surveys at 26 trading firms and nine FCMs. — [FIA](https://fia.org/fia/articles/fia-responds-cftc-concept-release-risk-controls-and-system-safeguards-automated)
- Kill-switch debate after Knight (JOURNALISM, snippets only). Markets Media, 4 October 2012, reports an SEC roundtable at which an exchange-level kill switch tied to a volume threshold was discussed; a Risk.net headline reports an expert warning that kill switches "may lead to systemic risk". [SNIPPET; not read]. — [Markets Media](https://www.marketsmedia.com/?p=16728); [Risk.net](https://risk.net/node/2303493)

**Banking IT change management: rollback plans and emergency changes:**

- Monetary Authority of Singapore, *Technology Risk Management Guidelines*, January 2021, §7.5 "Change Management". [FULL-TEXT]. ¶7.5.5: the FI should "establish a rollback plan to revert the information asset to the previous state if a problem arises during or after the change implementation." — [MAS TRM Guidelines 2021](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/MAS/Regulations-and-Financial-Stability/Regulatory-and-Supervisory-Framework/Risk-Management/TRM-Guidelines-18-January-2021.pdf)

### Inferences

- Trading rules already contain the essay's argument in miniature: a mandated kill capability (RTS 6 Art. 12), mandated limited initial exposure (Art. 8), and pre-set automated limits under the firm's own control (15c3-5). Payments has operational analogues (suspending a rail, a merchant or a flow; velocity limits) but I found no payments regulation text imposing an Article-12-style kill capability.
- On "is a rollback a change?": the texts read are silent. MAS places the rollback plan inside the original change; EBA dropped prescriptive wording and requires only "adequate safeguards" for emergencies; MAS requires that emergency approvers be identified. A bank policy that (i) approves the rollback procedure with the original change, (ii) classes its execution as a pre-authorised standard change, (iii) names the on-call engineer and incident commander as the authorisers for emergency undo, and (iv) requires logging plus after-the-fact review is consistent with all three texts. That is my synthesis; no regulator says it in terms.
- Impact tolerances give managers a regulatory hook for rollback speed: if a payment service's tolerance is measured in hours, a rollback path that needs a CAB convened or a multi-hour rebuild consumes much of it. This is an argument from the rule's structure, not a statement in PS21/3.
- RTS 6 Articles 5(2) and 11 keep human authorisation for deployment and material change. The pattern "designated approval to deploy, immediate capability to kill" is an asymmetry the regulation itself adopts: turning exposure up is controlled; turning it off must be immediate.

### Gaps

- No first-person account from a bank or payments firm of how rollback is classified under its change policy (pre-approved standard change, break-glass with retrospective review, or otherwise). One search returned only generic ITSM material. This is the most important gap for the essay.
- FFIEC current booklet text (Development, Acquisition and Maintenance) not read; site returned 403. The archived wording is snippet-only.
- SEC Regulation SCI (adopted November 2014) and the CFTC's Regulation AT proposals were not read; a secondary commentary (snippet) says Reg SCI does not require kill switches. Unverified.
- FCA Handbook SYSC 15A rule text could not be fetched (page rendered empty); only the PS21/3 landing page was read. PRA equivalents not read.
- EBA/GL/2019/04 may have been superseded or narrowed for many firms by the EU Digital Operational Resilience Act from January 2025; I did not verify current status.
- Payment-system equivalents (scheme rules on suspending participants, circuit breakers, velocity limits): no primary source located in this pass.
- The Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago paper referred to by FIA PTG was not read.

## 6. The human side: hesitation, blame, training, authority gradient, and the andon analogy

### Takeaway

Google's sources describe the hesitation directly (the pull to patch and "roll forward", the fear of being asked why) and answer it with norms: rollbacks are normal, no one asks why, practise on real tooling, reward the people who mitigate. Evidence that psychological safety or drills causally shorten time to rollback is assertion and anecdote, not measurement; and the andon-cord analogy is used far more in secondary commentary than in the primary engineering sources read here.

### Cited Findings

- Hesitation and "fix forward". Hilton 2017. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. Developers are tempted "to build a quick patch and then 'roll forward'"; Google generally advises against; "Under this kind of pressure, coding, testing or deployment mistakes are almost inevitable." The extraction also reports the article says a rollback request is not an attack on the author. — [Google Cloud Blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons)
- Blame. *Site Reliability Engineering*, ch. 15 "Postmortem Culture". [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "Blameless postmortems are a tenet of SRE culture." Without it, "people will not bring issues to light for fear of punishment." — [SRE book ch. 15](https://sre.google/sre-book/postmortem-culture/)

### Inferences

- The management levers the sources actually name are few and concrete: (1) say out loud that rollback is normal and will not be questioned; (2) require only a written reason after the fact; (3) make the tool a one-step action everyone on the rota has used; (4) rehearse with realistic drills; (5) put the reward system behind people who mitigate fast; (6) keep the change author reachable, or revert in their absence.
- The authority-gradient problem (a junior on-call engineer reversing a senior author's change) is addressed directly only by PagerDuty's vendor guide (rank is suspended during an incident) and indirectly by Google's "no-one will ask why". I found no primary account of a junior engineer hesitating or being overruled, so the essay should present the gradient as a recognised risk with a documented countermeasure, not as a measured phenomenon.
- If the essay uses the andon cord, it should be attributed carefully: Amazon applies the term to customer service in a shareholder letter; its use for deployments is a commentator's analogy. Booking.com's "Everyone in the company must be able and feel empowered to stop harmful experiments" is the closest primary statement of the andon idea, without the word.
- The Workbook case study where a halted release was restarted before root cause was known shows the opposite human failure: not hesitation to roll back, but impatience to resume. Authority to stop should be easy; authority to resume should require understanding.

### Gaps

- No empirical study was found measuring hesitation to roll back, sunk-cost effects, or the effect of blameless review on time-to-mitigate. All evidence here is practitioner assertion.
- Etsy's and Netflix's own words on who may revert were not read by me (403). Toyota primary sources on the andon cord were not consulted.
- DORA's State of DevOps reports were not re-read for restore-time findings on "who can act"; the 2.6x low-performer figure for external approval appeared only in secondary summaries and is not asserted.
- Game days and chaos exercises (Netflix, Amazon GameDay) were not researched in this pass.

## 7. Measured outcomes

### Takeaway

Hard numbers are scarce. The best are Slack's (73 percent of customer-facing incidents triggered by its own changes; customer impact hours down 90 percent from peak, with the drop following automatic rollbacks in April 2024) and Google's long-standing "roughly 70% of outages are due to changes". I did not find a primary Google statement, with wording I could verify, that "most outages are mitigated by rollback", nor any before/after time-to-mitigate figures from a bank.

### Cited Findings

- Google, *Site Reliability Engineering*, ch. 1 "Introduction" (Benjamin Treynor Sloss), 2016. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "SRE has found that roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system." No dataset, period or definition is given. — [SRE book ch. 1](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)
- Same chapter: playbooks give "roughly a 3x improvement in MTTR" over improvising. No dataset. — [SRE book ch. 1](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)
- Slack, Bailey, 7 October 2025. [TOOL-EXTRACT]. "The increasing majority (73%) of customer facing incidents were triggered by Slack-induced change". — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)
- Same post: "Customer impact hours are reduced from the peak by 90% and continuing to trend downward." Chart caption: "a 90% decrease in customer impact hours from Feb 2024 to Jan 2025", highlighting the drop "after automatic rollbacks were introduced Apr 2024." — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)
- Same post: "Proving success with many automatic rollbacks keeping customer impact below 10 minutes." Targets were 10 minutes automated, 20 minutes manual. — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)
- Limits of the Slack figures: self-reported by the programme's manager; "customer impact hours" is Slack's own metric; the programme included many projects besides automatic rollback, so the 90 percent is not attributable to rollback alone; the peak is the baseline, which flatters the comparison. — [Slack Engineering](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)

### Inferences

- Two independent large-company figures (Google about 70 percent in 2016, Slack 73 percent in 2025) agree that roughly seven in ten incidents are change-triggered. If most incidents come from a recent change, undoing the most recent change is the highest-yield first move even before diagnosis; this is the quantitative basis for "roll back first". Both figures are self-reported without methods.
- The measured benefit of empowered or automatic rollback is in impact duration, not in incident count. Slack's targets (10 and 20 minutes) and the Azure DevOps counter-example (45 plus 66 minutes of delay inside a 7.5-hour outage) bracket what is at stake.
- The Workbook's point that canary and naive deployments have similar detection and rollback times is worth stating to managers: ramps limit how many customers are hurt while rollback authority limits for how long. The two are complements, not substitutes.

### Gaps

- "Most outages are mitigated by rolling back a change" as a Google statement: not located with verifiable wording. The verified Google statements are that about 70 percent of outages are caused by changes and that rollback is a generic mitigation. Do not attribute the stronger claim to Google on the basis of these notes.
- No before/after time-to-mitigate series from Amazon, Monzo, Uber, Netflix or any bank. No share-of-incidents-mitigated-by-rollback figure from any source read.
- Ben Maurer, "Fail at Scale" (ACM Queue, 2015) is commonly cited for incident-cause data at Facebook; it could not be fetched (403 at both ACM Queue and CACM) and nothing is claimed from it.
- DORA time-to-restore distributions by approval regime were not re-read in this pass (see `empirical_and_regulated.md`).
