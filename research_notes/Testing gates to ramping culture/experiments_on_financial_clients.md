# Experiments and percentage rollouts on customers of financial services: practice, constraints and honest framing (c. 2010-2025)

Research date: 10 November 2026. Scope: payments, banking, lending, brokerage. Other researchers cover TSB 2018, Knight Capital, Stripe online migrations, Capital One pipeline gates, Monzo deploy frequency and the QCon 2019 Starling/Monzo panel (see `empirical_and_regulated.md`, `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`, `chart_data.md`); those are cross-referenced only.

- **[FULL-TOOL]**: the page was fetched in full, but I saw it through a fetch tool that returns a model-written digest with short quotations. **Three corrections the essay writer should know before using these notes:**

1. **SR 11-7 is no longer current.** The Federal Reserve, OCC and FDIC issued SR 26-2 on 17 April 2026, which "supersedes and replaces SR letter 11-7" [FULL-PDF]. The SR 11-7 attachment now returns 404 on federalreserve.gov and occ.gov, so I could not read its text this session. An essay about 2010-2025 can still cite SR 11-7 as the guidance in force for that period, but should say it was replaced in 2026.
2. **Neither "champion" nor "challenger" nor "parallel" appears in SR 26-2** (text search of the full PDF). The term that does appear is "benchmarking to other models". I could not confirm whether SR 11-7 used "champion/challenger"; I believe it did not, and that the phrase is industry and vendor vocabulary rather than regulator text. Do not write "regulator-recognised champion/challenger" on the strength of SR 11-7 without checking the 2011 text.
3. **Most named firms have no retrievable first-person account of customer experimentation.** Solid first-person material exists for Monzo, Adyen, Stripe (merchant-controlled testing and account-level versioning), Nubank (statistics only), Capital One (web UI only), Wise (one post, snippet only), Uber, Coinbase (snippet only) and GitHub. For Revolut, Starling, Chime, Cash App, PayPal, Klarna, Affirm, Robinhood, Intuit, American Express, JPMorgan, Marcus, Wealthfront, Betterment, Brex and Plaid I found job adverts or nothing. The essay should not imply that "all the fintechs publish how they A/B test customers".

## 1. Which financial firms have publicly documented experimentation or percentage rollouts on real customers, and how do they do it?

### Takeaway

The best-documented case is Monzo, whose product director describes a fixed ladder (staff, opt-in Labs, randomised experiment, staged percentage rollout, silent release) and a credit-product experiment run specifically to check for customer harm. Published experiments at financial firms are overwhelmingly on UI, support flows, communications and payment-optimisation settings; I found no first-person account of randomised experiments on price, credit decisions or ledger behaviour, and almost none that state guardrail thresholds or what is off limits.

### Cited Findings

**Monzo (UK bank) — the fullest first-person account:**

- Ed Stuart-Bourne (Product Director), "How we launch new products at Monzo", Monzo blog, 3 August 2023. [FULL-TOOL]. Describes the stages in order: research and prototyping with a few customers; staff testing; Monzo Labs (opt-in beta for engaged users); experiments with random assignment and a control group; staged rollouts; a silent period of a week or two before promotion; then marketing. The only percentage given is illustrative (10% on day one, 20% on day two). Staged rollouts come with a "clear plan to roll back if necessary". The post notes staff may not represent other customers. Limits: no experiment sizes, durations, guardrail thresholds or pause criteria; no discussion of regulation or of a different process for regulated products. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/08/03/how-we-launch-new-products-at-monzo)
- Same post, on a lending product: for a change to Monzo Flex (a credit product) payment dates, Monzo ran an experiment "to make sure it didn't lead to bad outcomes", such as customers struggling to make payments. This is the one place I found a bank describing a randomised experiment on a credit product used as a harm check rather than as a growth test. Limits: no metric definitions, sample or result. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/08/03/how-we-launch-new-products-at-monzo)
- Same post records a rollout that went wrong: after a transition screen disrupted customers, Monzo says it would have moved "a little more cautiously" had it considered the impact more deeply. Complicating evidence: a firm with a ramp ladder still shipped a disruptive change. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/08/03/how-we-launch-new-products-at-monzo)
- Monzo staff member "Bee", Monzo community forum, 20 April 2023: "every Monzo feature is rolled out to staff first who test it in everyday life." [FULL-TOOL]. Limits: a forum reply, not policy; "every" is an informal claim. A non-staff member on the same thread says most things roll out by percentage; that is not a company statement. — [Monzo community](https://community.monzo.com/t/what-is-monzos-rollout-policy/146669)
- Shnay Chohan (Lead Product Manager), "What we learned from 43 experiments in 12 months", Monzo blog, 28 March 2023. [FULL-TOOL]. "Over the last year we launched 43 experiments." The experiments were on customer-support self-serve flows (chat form, Help-tab quick links, search suggestions) and a fraud-reporting flow whose clearer wording "resulted in over £1m in savings each year". Key and guardrail metrics were fixed each half-year and reused; an example hypothesis pairs customer effort score with "maintaining the cost per weekly active user". "The control and enabled groups do not mix or overlap." Some hypotheses were disproved. Limits: support squad only; no pricing or lending; no population sizes; no success rate; no regulatory discussion. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/03/28/what-we-learned-from-43-experiments-in-12-months)
- Monzo, "How We Build the Monzo App", 2 March 2018: unfinished changes are hidden behind feature flags so code ships without customer access, and a flag can later be removed without a new app release. [SNIPPET]. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2018/03/02/app-development)
- Monzo, "How anyone at Monzo can test", 12 April 2022: staff sometimes get early access for a week or two before a big launch. [SNIPPET]. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/04/12/how-anyone-at-monzo-can-test)
- A secondary summary says Monzo formed an Experimentation Platform team in December 2023 that doubled company-wide experiment volume (InfoQ write-up of Fabien Deshayes' QCon London 2025 talk). [SNIPPET, JOURNALISM]; figures not verified against Monzo. — [InfoQ](https://www.infoq.com/news/2025/04/qcon-london-devex-monzo)

**Adyen (payments processor) — experiments on authorisation optimisation, and merchant-run experiments:**

- Rodel van Rooijen (Data Scientist, Adyen), "Optimizing payment conversion rates with contextual multi-armed bandits", 2 November 2020. [FULL-TOOL]. Adyen chooses among "hundreds of optimizations" per payment: whether to apply 3D Secure, card account updater for subscriptions, formatting message fields to bank preference, instant retries after temporary bank failures. The earlier approach was A/B tests optimised per account (merchant) and per bank; these became too thin when more payment features were added, so Adyen moved to an epsilon-greedy contextual bandit with weekly retraining. The exploration share is a tunable parameter with no value stated. Merchants using the product "automatically use" the optimisation. Results are qualitative only (one merchant's new policy needed time to beat the old one). Limits: no uplift numbers, no guardrails, no description of merchant consent or notice. — [Adyen](https://www.adyen.com/knowledge-hub/optimizing-payment-conversion-rates-with-contextual-multi-armed-bandits)
- Adyen documentation, "Uplift experiments" (current docs, undated). [FULL-TOOL, VENDOR documentation of its own product]. The merchant, not Adyen, starts the experiment, and needs a specific role (for example Risk admin for risk experiments). Testable items: temporarily removing a payment method in a country, payment-method ordering goal, a recommended optimisation setting, or two risk profiles. Traffic share is chosen by the merchant and can be changed while running; at 20% traffic each variant gets 10%. Primary methods such as cards cannot be removed in a checkout experiment. — [Adyen docs](https://docs.adyen.com/uplift/uplift-experiments)
- Adyen documentation, "Run A/B test experiments" (risk). [FULL-TOOL, VENDOR]. Adyen recommends "letting an experiment run for at least one month" so fraud and chargeback data mature; only one risk experiment per merchant account at a time; metrics are authorised %, refused by issuer %, blocked by risk %, fraud notifications %, chargebacks. This is a concrete financial-domain reason a ramp cannot be judged in hours: the outcome signal (chargebacks) arrives weeks later. — [Adyen docs](https://docs.adyen.com/risk-management/run-ab-test-experiments)
- A third-party summary of an Adyen paper says 58% of Adyen's internal A/B tests were flat or inconclusive. [SNIPPET, secondary aggregator]; not verified against an Adyen publication. — [chatpaper summary](https://chatpaper.com/paper/101743)

**Stripe (payments processor):**

- Steve Young and Janani Varadharajan (Stripe), "How we built it: Payment method A/B testing", 12 November 2024. [FULL-TOOL]. A merchant-facing tool: the merchant picks control and treatment payment methods and a traffic split from 0.01% to 99.99%; Stripe assigns each buyer by deterministic hash into 10,000 buckets. Stripe also says "we run A/B tests and share our insights" about checkout. Reported customer results: Indiegogo a 2% conversion increase. Limits: no guardrail or stopping rules described; it is the merchant who decides to experiment on the merchant's own buyers. — [Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/how-we-built-it-payment-method-a-b-testing)
- Ryan Drapeau (Stripe), "How we built it: Stripe Radar", 29 March 2023. [FULL-TOOL]. Describes offline evaluation only: features are prototyped to measure model impact; dropping the XGBoost component was rejected because of "a 1.5% drop in" recall. Radar blocks about 0.1% of legitimate payments. The post does not mention shadow mode, online A/B tests, per-merchant ramps or production guardrails. So this post does not support a claim that Stripe publicly documents shadow or A/B rollout of Radar models. — [Stripe](https://stripe.dev/blog/how-we-built-it-stripe-radar)
- Brandur Leach (former Stripe engineer, personal site), "Flags v. gates", 18 November 2021. [FULL-TOOL]. At Stripe, rollout and operational switches were "feature flags", which could be on for a list of IDs (usually account IDs), a random percentage, or a stable percentage by ID; deprecated behaviour and internal settings were "gates", and "Gates conversely were stored on an account." Limits: a personal recollection, not a Stripe publication; no counts; does not describe beta programmes. — [brandur.org](https://brandur.org/fragments/flags-v-gates)

**Nubank (Brazil, digital bank):**

- Nubank Experimentation Platform Data Science team (Borges, Morais, Assunção, Rossi, Vilarino), "3 lessons from implementing ... CUPED at Nubank", 25 November 2025. [FULL-TOOL]. Confirms a central experimentation platform running A/B tests across "millions of customers and hundreds of metrics", with Bonferroni corrections on multi-metric dashboards. Limits: a statistics post; it does not say what is tested (credit limits, price, UI), how populations are chosen, or what is excluded. — [Nubank](https://building.nu.com/3-lessons-from-implementing-controlled-experiment-using-pre-experiment-data-cuped-at-nubank/)
- CNCF, "Nubank case study", 10 July 2020. [FULL-TOOL; a foundation case study with Nubank quotations, not a Nubank engineering post]. During the Kubernetes migration, "The new shard took on about 20 customers for testing", then "we started to migrate the other shards, one by one". "Today, Nubank engineers are deploying 700 times a week." Director of Engineering Renan Capaverde credits canary deployments with easier rollback. This is an infrastructure ramp by customer shard, not a product experiment. — [CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/case-studies/nubank/)
- A Nubank meetup recap says the mobile app release goes to 5% of customers first and scales to 100% over several days. [SNIPPET]. — [Nubank](https://building.nubank.com/platform-engineering-observability-and-scaling-mobile-development)

**Wise (TransferWise):**

- Wise Engineering, "How product engineers build new features at TransferWise" (Medium; date not retrieved). [SNIPPET; the page returned 403]. ### Inferences

- What financial firms publish about experimenting is concentrated in low-stakes surfaces: app UI, help and support flows, marketing pages, communications, checkout payment-method presentation, and optimisation of authorisation plumbing. The silence about price, credit decision and ledger experiments is itself informative, though silence is not proof of absence.
- Monzo's ladder separates two things the essay wants to separate: the randomised "experiment" (to learn whether a feature is used and sustainable) and the "staged rollout" (to limit blast radius, with a rollback plan). Monzo lists them as different stages with different purposes.
- Adyen's one-month recommendation for risk experiments shows why a short ramp is not sufficient in finance: fraud and chargeback outcomes lag by weeks. A canary can catch crashes and declines in minutes; it cannot catch a fraud regression. This complicates "replace multi-day soak with a ramp" for any change whose harm shows up in delayed financial outcomes.
- In the Adyen and Stripe products, the party who consents to and controls the experiment is the merchant. The processor supplies the mechanism; it does not randomly vary a merchant's checkout on its own initiative (Adyen's 2020 bandit is the exception, and it sits inside a product the merchant has adopted).

### Gaps

- No first-person engineering account retrieved for Revolut, Starling, Klarna, Affirm, Coinbase (experimentation), Intuit, American Express, JPMorgan Chase digital, Goldman Sachs Marcus, Wealthfront, Betterment, Brex or Plaid on customer experimentation. One combined search was run for several of these; a per-company search might find more. `qa_role_transitions.md` section 3 already covers Wealthfront's deployment practice.
- No firm in this set publishes guardrail thresholds, auto-shutdown rules, or a list of what is never experimented on.
- No retrievable Stripe account of shadow or A/B rollout of Radar models, and no Stripe post on per-merchant percentage rollouts.
- The Wise post could not be opened; all details are from search summaries.
- The Adyen "58% inconclusive" figure is unverified.

## 2. Is per-client, per-merchant or per-account activation the finance-native ramp?

### Takeaway

Yes for the business-to-business platforms where I found evidence: Stripe pins API behaviour to the account and lets the account choose when to move; Adyen exposes experiments as a per-merchant-account setting the merchant turns on; Nubank ramps infrastructure by customer shard; Wise reportedly ramps by currency and country. The unit of exposure is a client, account, shard or corridor the firm can name and notify, not an anonymous random percentage of requests.

### Cited Findings

- Brandur Leach (Stripe, API Experience), "APIs as infrastructure: future-proofing Stripe with versioning", Stripe blog, 5 August 2017. [FULL-TOOL]. "The first time a user makes an API request, their account is automatically pinned to the most recent version available." Later calls use that version unless a `Stripe-Version` header overrides it; the user upgrades from the dashboard. "Version change modules keep older API versions abstracted out of core code paths." Limits: about backwards-incompatible API changes, not about ramping internal changes; the post does not mention gates or flags. — [Stripe](https://stripe.com/blog/api-versioning)
- Stripe documentation, "API upgrades": for 72 hours after upgrading its API version an account can roll back, and failed webhooks are retried in the old structure after rollback. [SNIPPET of current docs]. This is a client-controlled change with a client-controlled reversal window. — [Stripe docs](https://docs.stripe.com/upgrades)
- Brandur Leach, "Flags v. gates", 2021: Stripe feature flags could target a list of account IDs or an ID-stable percentage; gates were stored on the account; ID lists became unworkable at "tens of thousands of users or more". [FULL-TOOL; personal recollection]. — [brandur.org](https://brandur.org/fragments/flags-v-gates)
- Adyen docs: experiments are created by a merchant user with a named role; only one risk experiment can be active per merchant account; transactions where the merchant explicitly assigns a risk profile in the request are excluded. [FULL-TOOL, VENDOR docs]. — [Adyen docs](https://docs.adyen.com/risk-management/run-ab-test-experiments)
- Adyen 2020: the pre-bandit A/B tests were grouped by account (merchant) and by issuing bank. [FULL-TOOL]. — [Adyen](https://www.adyen.com/knowledge-hub/optimizing-payment-conversion-rates-with-contextual-multi-armed-bandits)
- Trade press reports an Adyen pilot of intelligent debit routing with 20 merchants, including eBay and 24 Hour Fitness, reporting acceptance costs down 26% and authorisation rate up 0.22% on average. [SNIPPET, JOURNALISM relaying company figures]. A named-merchant pilot before general release. — [Digital Transactions](https://www.digitaltransactions.net/adyen-rolls-out-intelligent-routing-in-response-to-debit-network-choice/)
- Nubank via CNCF, 2020: one shard with about 20 customers first, then the other shards one at a time. [FULL-TOOL]. — [CNCF](https://www.cncf.io/case-studies/nubank/)
- Wise: gradual A/B rollout "across currencies or countries". [SNIPPET]. — [Wise Engineering](https://medium.com/wise-engineering/how-product-engineers-build-new-features-at-transferwise-88752cf87fa)
- Monzo: staff first, then opt-in Labs, before any random percentage. [FULL-TOOL]. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/08/03/how-we-launch-new-products-at-monzo)
- Market infrastructure uses whole-client rehearsal rather than partial exposure. London Stock Exchange publishes "Customer Dress Rehearsal" guides for functional releases, whose purpose is to verify that customer applications can handle the changes. [SNIPPET of an LSEG service announcement dated 2026; the practice is long-standing but I did not retrieve an older notice]. — [LSEG](https://www.lseg.com/content/dam/lseg/en_us/documents/londonstockexchange-backup/live-001-08072026-london-stock-exchange-next-functional-release-1-26-9-customer-dress-rehearsal-cdr-guide.pdf)
- Federal Reserve Financial Services, Fedwire ISO 20022: a single-day cutover on 10 March 2025, preceded by Saturday production "Dress Rehearsal" testing on select Saturdays in January and February 2025. [SNIPPET]. A shared network could not ramp by percentage because all participants must speak the same message format on the same day; it rehearsed in production instead. — [FRB Services](https://frbservices.org/resources/financial-services/wires/iso-20022-implementation-center/fedwire-iso-20022-testing-requirements-key-milestones)
- DTCC T+1: industry testing ran from 14 August 2023 to 31 May 2024 in bi-weekly cycles, optional but "highly recommended". [SNIPPET]. — [DTCC](https://www.dtcc.com/insights/2023/industrys-t1-testing-window-opens)

### Inferences

- For a core-banking or payments platform with institutional clients, the honest translation of "ramp to 1%, 10%, 50%" is "staff or house accounts, then a named friendly client, then client by client, corridor by corridor". The evidence above supports that this is how the documented platforms actually do it.
- Account pinning (Stripe) is the opposite of a silent percentage ramp: the client knows which behaviour it has and chooses when to change. It supports the essay's point that notice obligations and gradual exposure are compatible, provided the ramp unit is the client.
- Fedwire ISO 20022 is a counter-example to "everything can be ramped": changes to a shared wire format among counterparties are flag days by nature. The risk reduction there came from repeated production rehearsals, which is closer to soak than to canary.

### Gaps

- No primary source found stating that financial clients contractually require notice of changes, though the Stripe versioning design implies it. A master services agreement or scheme rule citation would be needed to assert it.
- No first-person card-network (Visa, Mastercard) account of pilot programmes was retrieved.
- No source describing how a core-banking vendor (FIS, Fiserv, Temenos, Thought Machine, Mambu) ramps a release across client banks.
- The LSEG evidence is a 2026 document; an earlier dated example was not retrieved.

## 3. What regulatory and ethical constraints apply when different customers get different behaviour?

### Takeaway

UK conduct regulation explicitly endorses randomised trials and A/B tests on real financial customers as the strongest way to check understanding, and the FCA has run such trials with firms since 2013; what it restricts is the purpose and the asymmetry (testing only to maximise sales, exploiting biases, unexplained different outcomes for groups). In the US the clearest precedent runs the other way: the FTC used Credit Karma's own A/B test results as evidence of knowledge in a deception case and ordered it to preserve testing records. Experiments on price, credit decisions and execution quality are where the evidence says to be most careful, and where I found no firm publishing its practice.

### Cited Findings

**UK FCA: the regulator endorses experiments, under conditions:**

- FCA, "FG22/5 Final non-Handbook Guidance for firms on the Consumer Duty", July 2022, para 8.44. [FULL-PDF]. Where testing is appropriate, "customers' level of understanding could be tested directly by conducting randomised controlled trials or A/B tests". Box 1 (after 8.44) ranks "randomised controlled trials or A/B tests with real customers" as a direct measure that "can be the best way" to measure a communication, while noting it needs statistical skill and a large enough sample. — [FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf)
- FG22/5 para 8.41 (repeated in chapter 8 examples). [FULL-PDF]. The symmetry test: "where they conduct consumer testing of communications to determine an effective approach to maximise sales, they should use testing capabilities of an equivalent standard" for consumer understanding. This is the single most useful sentence for an engineering audience: if you can A/B test for conversion, the regulator expects you to A/B test for comprehension and outcomes too. — [FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf)
- FG22/5 para 8.42. [FULL-PDF]. "The rules under this outcome require firms to test communications where appropriate." The underlying rule is PRIN 2A.5.10R. [SNIPPET for the Handbook rule text]. — [FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf); [FCA Handbook PRIN 2A.5](https://handbook.fca.org.uk/handbook/prin2a/prin2as5)
- FG22/5 chapter 7 (price and value), list of evidence firms can use to assess value. [FULL-PDF]. Includes "retention rates or relevant A/B testing of variation in product or service design". A/B test results are named as legitimate evidence in fair-value assessments. — [FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf)
- FG22/5 para 6.36. [FULL-PDF]. "Firms should satisfy themselves that different outcomes for different groups of customers are compatible with the firm fully meeting the standards required by the Duty". Para 7.37: charging different prices to different groups is "not necessarily in breach of the Duty", with a pointer to the FCA's fair-pricing framework. Differential treatment is permitted but must be justified and monitored. — [FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf)
- FG22/5 on sludge and bias (chapter 2 summary and consumer-support chapter). [FULL-PDF]. Firms should "not seek to exploit customers' behavioural biases"; good support "includes avoiding 'sludge' in the design of consumer journeys, which uses friction to prevent consumers from taking actions". The guidance does not prohibit testing; it prohibits a class of results being shipped. — [FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf)
- Erta, Hunt, Iscenko and Brambley, "Applying behavioural economics at the Financial Conduct Authority", FCA Occasional Paper No. 1, April 2013, section on gathering evidence and Boxes 6-7 (around pp. 40-45). [FULL-PDF]. "Randomised Controlled Trials (RCTs) offer one of the most direct ways of getting evidence on consumer choices." Box 7 describes an RCT on redress letters: "We worked with a firm that was voluntarily contacting customers about a failing in its sales process." Seven letter treatments were applied at random against the firm's control letter. Limits: the paper carries a disclaimer that the views are the authors' and not the FCA's; it is about the regulator's own research method, not a licence for firms. — [FCA OP1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-1.pdf)
- FCA Occasional Paper No. 2, "Encouraging consumers to claim redress", April 2013: the trial involved a firm writing to almost 200,000 customers. [SNIPPET]. — [FCA OP2](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-2.pdf)
- Adams, Hunt, Vale and Zaliauskas (FCA Chief Economist's Department), Occasional Paper No. 7, "Stimulating interest: Reminding savers to act when rates decrease", 20 January 2015. [FULL-TOOL of the landing page, not the paper]. A randomised controlled trial with over 20,000 savings customers; reminders raised switching by 5.6 to 7.9 percentage points, measured 20 weeks after the rate cut. "The fact of getting a reminder was more important than its precise phrasing." Note the control group here received no reminder and so switched less: a regulator-run trial in which the control arm was financially worse off, accepted as the price of learning. — [FCA OP7](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-no-7-stimulating-interest-reminding-savers-act-when)
- FCA, "Consumer understanding: good practice and areas for improvement", 13 March 2026 (outside the 2010-2025 window but the most recent regulator statement). [FULL-TOOL]. Good practice lists "short surveys, comprehension checks, A/B testing or customer callbacks" as proportionate methods, and testing "both before and after changes". Poor practice: firms that "continued to rely on sales data or the absence of complaints as evidence of understanding"; testing that was "often superficial, one-off or poorly documented". — [FCA](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/good-and-poor-practice/consumer-understanding-good-practice-areas-improvement)
- FCA, "Digital design in customers' online journeys: good practice and areas for improvement" (consumer credit firms; date not captured). [FULL-TOOL]. ### Inferences

- The FCA position can be stated accurately as: experiments on real customers are endorsed for learning whether communications and journeys work for customers; the firm must hold its comprehension and outcome testing to the same standard as its sales testing; and it must be able to justify different outcomes for different groups. That is a stronger pro-experiment statement than most engineers expect from a financial regulator, and it is in primary text.
- The Credit Karma order shows the other edge. Experiment records are discoverable, and a result showing that a misleading variant converts better is evidence of knowledge. An experimentation programme in a regulated firm needs a rule about what variants may be tested at all, and should expect its logs to be read by a regulator.
- Equipoise (from the Kohavi index, a term borrowed from clinical trials) maps cleanly onto the essay's distinction. In a product experiment the firm genuinely does not know which arm is better for the customer, and both arms are acceptable. In a safety ramp the firm believes the new version is at least as good and is limiting exposure in case it is wrong. Neither is acceptable if the firm already believes one arm is worse for the customer on a matter where it owes a duty (price fairness, credit decision, execution quality).
- Where experiments appear restricted or inadvisable on the evidence here: (a) randomised variation of price or credit terms across otherwise identical customers, because of fair-value, price-discrimination and fair-lending scrutiny, and because no firm publishes doing it; (b) randomised order routing where one arm is expected to be worse, because best execution is owed order by order; (c) any variant that would be deceptive if shipped, since testing it creates the evidence; (d) journeys for credit where speed or defaults are the treatment, given the FCA's digital-design findings; (e) exposure of vulnerable customers without monitoring outcomes for that group.
- A purely technical ramp (same intended customer outcome) raises none of these differential-treatment issues by design, provided it is truly outcome-neutral. That is the honest basis for calling a canary something other than an experiment on customers.

## 4. Which older finance traditions are effectively the same idea?

### Takeaway

Parallel running is written into bank capital regulation (at least four quarters computing old and new capital ratios side by side before the new one counts), and phased migration by product or customer batch is how several large core replacements were actually delivered. The record is not one-sided: the Commonwealth Bank's phased programme overran heavily, Fedwire's ISO 20022 change was necessarily a single-day cutover, and one phased integration (Lloyds/HBOS) included a reported "big bang" online-banking merge that caused customer access problems.

During it the bank stays under the general risk-based capital rules but "would simultaneously calculate its risk-based capital ratios under the advanced approaches" and reports both quarterly. ### Inferences

- The Basel parallel run is the strongest "native to finance" example because it is in regulatory text and has the exact structure of a shadow launch: the new calculation runs on live data for at least a year, both results are reported, and only the old one binds. It is also a year long, which cuts against the essay's "replace multi-day soak" framing. An honest use is: finance already accepts running new alongside old on production inputs; the argument is about doing it continuously and automatically, not about shortening it for high-consequence models.
- Phased migration reduces the size of each failure, not the cost of the programme. CBA is evidence for both halves of that sentence.
- Dress rehearsals and industry test windows are the tradition for changes that cannot be ramped because every counterparty must change together. The essay should concede this class exists.

## 5. Purely technical experiments on ledgers and payment systems: what did they report?

### Takeaway

Shadow comparison of old and new code or stores on production traffic, with the old result returned to the caller, is documented for a payments ledger at Uber (trillion-entry migration, 2024), at Coinbase (2020, snippet only) and in the origin of GitHub's Scientist library. The accounts that give numbers show it is slow by nature: Uber's own arithmetic says proving six nines takes more than a day of traffic and seven nines about twelve days, live shadowing missed cold data entirely, and the whole migration took about two years.

### Cited Findings

**Uber LedgerStore (payments ledger):**

- Raghav Gautam, Erik Seaberg and Abhishek Kanhar, "Migrating a Trillion Entries of Uber's Ledger Data from DynamoDB to LedgerStore", Uber Engineering blog, 11 April 2024. [FULL-TOOL]. Shadow validation duplicated production traffic to the new store and compared responses with the old path; the bar was "at least 99.99% complete and correct as measured by shadow validation", with an upper bound of six nines to cap investigation effort. The post's arithmetic: six nines needs about 100 million comparisons, a bit more than a day at 1,000 records per second; seven nines would take about 12 days; and at six nines slightly over one million records could still be corrupt in a trillion. — [Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/migrating-from-dynamodb-to-ledgerstore/)
- Same post, limits of shadowing. [FULL-TOOL]. Live shadow traffic does not exercise rarely read cold data, so Uber added offline validation comparing full LedgerStore data against DynamoDB dumps; the largest single Spark job compared 760 billion records (about 70 TB compressed). Offline validation found missing records and drove incremental backfills. Eventually consistent indexes produced false-positive mismatches. — [Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/migrating-from-dynamodb-to-ledgerstore/)
- Same post, cutover. [FULL-TOOL]. Rolled out "over a few weeks" with sign-off from on-call engineers of major callers; reads first ran with a fallback to DynamoDB for records missing in LedgerStore, and fallback logs were reviewed record by record before the fallback was removed; DynamoDB was kept for about a month after writes stopped. The migration took about two years with no downtime reported. Limits: no observed mismatch rate is published, only targets; ramp percentages are not given; self-reported. — [Uber](https://www.uber.com/blog/migrating-from-dynamodb-to-ledgerstore/); [InfoQ summary](https://www.infoq.com/news/2024/05/uber-dynamodb-ledgerstore)

**Coinbase internal ledger:**

- Alex Ghise, "The art of a seamless migration of Coinbase's internal ledger", Coinbase blog, 2020. [SNIPPET; both the Medium and coinbase.com copies returned 403]. Search summaries say writes went to both legacy and new systems, reads were performed against both, discrepancies were logged, and the legacy result was returned, in phases starting legacy-only. No numbers were retrieved. — [Coinbase blog](https://medium.com/the-coinbase-blog/the-art-of-a-seamless-migration-of-coinbases-internal-ledger-76c4c0d5f028)

**GitHub Scientist (origin of the technique's best-known library):**

- Jesse Toth, "Scientist: Measure Twice, Cut Once", GitHub blog, 3 February 2016. [FULL-TOOL]. Scientist was built to verify a rewrite of permissions code, "one of the most critical systems in our application". The control's result is returned to the caller; the candidate runs alongside in randomised order; mismatches and timings are recorded; candidate exceptions are swallowed. The experiment can be enabled for a percentage of calls and the post advises ramping slowly because of the performance cost. Limits: no aggregate mismatch numbers. — [GitHub](https://github.blog/2016-02-03-scientist/)
- Vicent Martí, "Move Fast and Fix Things", GitHub blog, 15 December 2015. [FULL-TOOL]. Replacing the merge implementation: started at 1% of requests and ramped; about four days of iteration then 24 hours at 100% with no mismatches and no slow cases; "tens of millions of merge commits" compared; the work is described as "roughly 5 days of part-time work". **Other systems checked:**

- Square, "Books, an immutable double-entry accounting database service", Square developer blog, 2019: introduces the ledger service; the search summary indicates it does not describe a shadow-compared migration. [SNIPPET]. — [Square](https://developer.squareup.com/blog/books-an-immutable-double-entry-accounting-database-service/)
- Monzo: posts on balance read performance (28 April 2023) and on processing payments safely (8 February 2022) exist, but the snippets do not show shadow or dark-read comparison. [SNIPPET]. — [Monzo](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/04/28/speeding-up-our-balance-read-time-the-planning-phase)

### Inferences

- These are experiments in the engineering sense only: the customer is meant to get the same answer on both arms, and in the shadow phase always gets the old answer. They carry none of the differential-treatment issues in section 3, which is the factual basis for the essay to treat them separately from A/B tests.
- Uber's arithmetic is a quantitative argument about soak. Confidence in a ledger change is bought with volume of comparisons, and at a given traffic rate that is a duration. Shadowing replaces calendar-driven soak with evidence-driven soak; it does not make the time go away for rare-case correctness. For a core-banking system with far lower request rates than Uber, reaching the same confidence by shadowing would take longer, not shorter.
- Shadow traffic is biased toward hot paths. Month-end, year-end, dormant accounts and rarely read history need a separate offline comparison. This matches the "known limits of canaries" theme in `counter_evidence_and_failures.md`.
- GitHub's merge case shows the pattern at its best (days, not months) but for a pure function. Writes with side effects, such as posting ledger entries or sending payment messages, cannot simply be run twice, which is why the ledger accounts use dual-write plus read comparison plus offline reconciliation.

- Coinbase's post could not be opened; no figures.
- Nothing retrieved for Wise's ledger, Nubank core rewrites, PayPal, Shopify payments ("Verdict" could not be confirmed as a Shopify library), Square Books migration, or an Airbnb payments-specific migration.
- Uber's 2019-2021 payments-platform posts were not retrieved; only the 2024 LedgerStore post.

## 6. How should "experiments" be framed so the essay's argument is honest?

### Takeaway

The sources support three distinct things that share machinery (a flag, a targeting rule, a comparison) but differ in what the customer is owed: a product experiment (two acceptable arms, genuine uncertainty about which customers prefer), a safety ramp (one intended outcome, limited exposure in case of regression), and a shadow or parallel run (no customer exposure at all). The essay's case is strongest for the second and third; for the first, financial regulators endorse it for communications and journeys and scrutinise it for price, credit and execution.

### Cited Findings

- Monzo treats "experiments" and "staged rollouts" as separate stages with separate purposes: the experiment has a control group and measures discoverability, usage and cost; the staged rollout has a rollback plan. ### Inferences

- Suggested vocabulary for the essay, each tied to evidence above:
  - **Shadow or parallel run**: new code computes on live inputs, old result is used. Customer exposure none. Finance precedent: Basel parallel run; Uber and Coinbase ledgers. Constraint: takes time proportional to the confidence wanted and misses cold paths.
  - **Safety ramp (canary, staged rollout, per-client activation)**: the new behaviour is intended to be equivalent or better for every customer; exposure is limited by staff, named client, shard, currency or percentage; a rollback plan exists. Finance precedent: Nubank shards, Monzo staged rollouts, Stripe account pinning. Constraint: only detects regressions whose signal arrives within the ramp; delayed outcomes (chargebacks, arrears, reconciliation breaks, month-end) need a longer hold.
  - **Product experiment (A/B test)**: two variants the firm would be willing to ship to anyone, randomised to learn which serves customers and the business better. Finance precedent: Monzo support and Flex experiments, FCA trials, FG22/5. Constraint: needs a rule on what may be varied, outcome metrics as well as conversion metrics, monitoring by customer group, and retained records.
- Calling a safety ramp an "experiment" in front of a risk or compliance audience invites the section 3 objections unnecessarily. Calling a product experiment a "rollout" hides that customers are being treated differently on purpose. The essay will be more accurate, and more persuasive to the people it addresses, if it uses separate words.
- The claim "release trains with multi-day soak should be replaced by flags, canaries and ramps" needs two qualifications from this evidence. First, for ledger-correctness and model changes, the finance tradition and the engineering accounts both use long observation windows (four quarters; two years; one month); the improvement on offer is that the observation happens on production inputs with a small or zero blast radius, not that it is short. Second, shared-protocol changes among counterparties (wire formats, settlement cycles) remain flag days with rehearsals.
- The claim that the practice is "native to finance rather than imported" is supportable for parallel running and phased migration, and for regulator-run randomised trials. It is not yet supportable from these notes for champion/challenger, which lacks a primary source here.
