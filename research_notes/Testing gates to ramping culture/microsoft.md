One caution that applies to the whole file: "Microsoft" is not one culture. The evidence comes from three distinct populations, on different timelines: (a) Bing / the Experimentation Platform (ExP, later Analysis & Experimentation), 2005 onward; (b) the Team Foundation Service / Visual Studio Online / VSTS / Azure DevOps team under Brian Harry, 2010 onward; (c) Office and Windows, which arrive later and with much longer bake times. Do not generalise from one to the others without saying so.

## Bing / Experimentation Platform (Kohavi and colleagues): cultural obstacles, managers' roles, and ramp-up / auto-shutdown as safety

### Takeaway

The ExP papers say plainly that the hard part was cultural and about incentives, not technical: people resisted a loss of decision authority, and goals rewarded "shipping" rather than measured impact. They also frame automated alerting and automatic shutdown of bad experiments as the "safety net" that replaces heavy up-front testing once the number of live variants makes classical testing infeasible; the gradual-ramp mechanism is described explicitly in the 2017 ICSE paper rather than in the 2009 or 2013 Kohavi papers.

### Cited Findings

Source register for this section:

- **S1.** Ron Kohavi, Thomas Crook, Roger Longbotham, "Online Experimentation at Microsoft," Third Workshop on Data Mining Case Studies and Practice Prize, 2009 (the PDF header says it won 3rd place there). [FULL-TEXT] via PDF-to-text; two-column extraction interleaves lines, so I quote only sentences that were contiguous. URL: <https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf>. Limits: authored by the platform team itself (advocacy); covers MSN and other web properties, not Windows/Office; contains no discussion of ramp-up, auto-abort or deployment safety (a search of the extracted text for "ramp" and "abort" found nothing).
- **S2.** Ron Kohavi, Alex Deng, Brian Frasca, Toby Walker, Ya Xu, Nils Pohlmann, "Online Controlled Experiments at Large Scale," KDD 2013 (the copy read is the "to appear at KDD 2013" author version, 9 pages). [FULL-TEXT] via PDF-to-text. URL: <https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf>. Limits: Bing only; revenue claims ("hundreds of millions of dollars") are the authors' own attribution, not audited; says nothing about test-role reorganisation.

Findings:

- Timeline of the practice (not the publication): Kohavi joined Microsoft in 2005; the ExP team formed in March 2006 as "a small incubation project" of seven people including one tester by end of summer; its mission was to build an easy-to-integrate platform and to "change the culture towards more data-driven decisions". (S1 §3) — [Kohavi, Crook, Longbotham 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Starting point: "In 2005, no Microsoft groups that we are aware of ran proper controlled experiments with statistical tests." Groups that ran "flights" split traffic but ran no significance tests or power calculations. (S1 §7.1, verbatim, 18 words) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Adoption was slow. Year one: two proof-of-concept experiments. Year two: advocacy and education, "a 'chasm' year" in which "only eight experiments ultimately ran successfully". Year three: about one new experiment a week; over 15 web properties had run at least one. (S1 §3) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Humbling base rate: of well-designed experiments intended to improve a key metric, "only about one-third were successful at improving the key metric"; elsewhere the paper says features that do not improve or actually hurt their target metric "account for 66% of experiments" at Microsoft. (S1 §5 and §7.4) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf). Restated in 2013: "Only one third of the ideas tested at Microsoft improved the metric(s) they were designed to improve". (S2 §2, Tenet 3, verbatim, 17 words) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- HiPPO: defined as "Highest Paid Person's Opinion"; the team handed out thousands of HiPPO stress toys at company meetings and classes as an awareness campaign; the paper says the campaign "went viral" internally. (S1 §7) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Education as a management task: a monthly one-day class on statistics and experiment design that initially "couldn't fill the class (of about 20)" and later had more than 500 attendees and a waiting list; an internal mailing list of over 700; posters ("Experiment or Die!"); and a guess-the-winner quiz of eight A/B tests in which, of over 200 respondents, nobody got 6 of 8 right and the mean was 2.3. (S1 §7.1-7.2) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Perceived loss of power (S1 §7.2): the paper cites Linsky and Heifetz that people resist loss rather than change, and says some people saw experimentation "as a risk to their power and/or prestige"; program managers choose features, and "Proposing several alternatives and admitting you don't know which is best is hard." (verbatim, 12 words) It also reports hearing "we know what to do. It's in our DNA". — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- A concrete escalation: a proposal to algorithmically optimise headlines on a major Microsoft content site was resisted by the US editorial team as "part of their job"; development stalled; the experiment ran outside the US and succeeded; "The issue escalated to higher management and the project got the go-ahead." (S1 §7.2) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Incentives (S1 §7.3 "Reward Systems"): "Most goals in software development organizations at Microsoft are around 'shipping' products, not about their impact on customers or key metrics." (verbatim, 21 words) The paper cites Lee et al. (2004) that "inconsistent reward systems that punish failure lead to aversion", and proposes tying goals to key metrics rather than to products and features. — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Candid limit on their own success: "it is unlikely that we have made a dent in many people's yearly performance goals. This is an ongoing challenge". (S1 §7.3, verbatim, 20 words) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Conceded cost: tying goals to metrics and testing ideas means "The feature development pace will undoubtedly slow down, but more corrections will be made on the way". (S1 §7.3, verbatim, 17 words) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Contrast with boxed-product culture: "Office releases are well planned and executed over three to four years"; a culture of "do it right the first time" finds launching prototypes and extending only those that prove useful "a challenging proposition". (S1 §7 and §5 item 3) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Stated limits of experiments (S1 §6.2, §7.4, §8): they need an agreed Overall Evaluation Criterion, easy data collection and easy change; they suit "strategy execution, not strategy formulation"; running a long-lived test deprives some users of the better variant and "maintaining the code for multiple versions of your site is expensive". — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)
- Scale by 2013: "over 200 concurrent experiments" at Bing on any day; about 100 million monthly US users; 90% of eligible users in over 15 concurrent experiments with 10% in a holdout; roughly 5^15 (about 30 billion) possible variants of the site. (S2 abstract and §1) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- The pre-release-testing claim most relevant to the essay: "Classical testing and debugging techniques no longer apply when there are billions of live variants of the site" (verbatim, 18 words), so alerts are used "rather than relying on heavy up-front testing". (S2 abstract) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- The four cultural stages, including the Semmelweis reflex (S2 §3.1): (1) Hubris, where measurement is not needed "because of confidence in the HiPPO"; (2) Measurement and Control; (3) Semmelweis Reflex, where "the organization rejects new knowledge because it contradicts entrenched norms, beliefs or paradigms" (verbatim, 13 words); (4) Fundamental understanding. — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- "Test everything" includes re-implementations that have passed all exit criteria (S2 §3.3): for a platform replacement that has "passed all exit criteria", "the final test has to be a controlled experiment". The Amazon order-pipeline anecdote (first author's memory): the rewritten pipeline failed an A/B test with a 2% revenue loss and "shipped after five iterations". Small fixes should be "bundled into packages so that if one is egregiously bad, the package will test negatively". — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Attitude to error: "The key is to admit that mistakes will happen and try to run controlled experiments to detect them." (S2 §3.3, verbatim, 18 words) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Alerts and aborting (S2 §4.3): the organisation needs "to automatically detect and alert when degradations occur and to automatically revert any changes that cause severe degradations"; "This use of controlled experiments provides a critical safety net" (verbatim, 10 words) that lets the company scale changes "while still maintaining a tolerable level of risk". Severity tiers: "The most severe changes result in automatic shutdown of an experiment" while less severe ones email the owner and a central team. — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Alerting had to be engineered against false alarms: naive alerting on any significant negative movement "will lead to an unacceptable number of false alerts"; they require practical as well as statistical significance (no alert on a 2 ms page-load regression) and use O'Brien-Fleming sequential thresholds (day-1 p-value cutoff 5e-8 in a 7-day experiment). (S2 §4.3) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Decentralised decisions, centralised platform (S2 §4): self-service tools "enable all engineers in the organization to run their own experiments"; "a small centralized team" provides tools, guidance and oversight while what to run and how to act "is largely decentralized". — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Divisions differed in 2013: "Office and Windows follow Sinofsky's long planning and execution cycles" while Bing, with "thousands of developers, program managers, and testers", used experiments to decide what to ship. Note that Bing still had testers as a named role at this date. (S2 §1) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- A seasonal freeze existed: Figure 2's caption notes "Prior to 2012, Bing shut down most experiments the last two weeks of December." (S2 Fig. 2) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Experiment duration is not instant: the final scorecard is required "to be a multiple of weeks, usually two weeks". (S2 §5) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)

### Inferences

- The papers support the claim that a manager's job changed from deciding what is good to funding a measurement platform, agreeing an evaluation criterion, and accepting being overruled by data. They do not show that managers' formal incentives actually changed; S1 says they probably had not by 2009.
- For the essay's audience, the strongest transferable point is S2's argument that when there are too many live configurations to test exhaustively, detection-and-revert in production becomes the primary control. The papers argue this for a search engine where a bad variant costs clicks and revenue for a fraction of users; they make no claim about domains where a single wrong output is irreversible (payments, ledgers). That gap is mine to flag, not the papers'.
- Bing's experiments run about two weeks for statistical reasons. "Ramping" in this literature shortens exposure to harm, not time-to-decision. An essay claiming ramps are faster than a three-day soak should not cite Bing for speed.

### Gaps

- I did not read Kohavi, Longbotham, Sommerfield, Henne, "Controlled experiments on the web: survey and practical guide" (2009) or the KDD 2007 "Practical Guide", which are the usual citations for ramp-up and auto-abort in the early ExP work. S1 and S2 cite them but I cannot quote them.
- I did not read Kohavi, Tang, Xu, *Trustworthy Online Controlled Experiments* (Cambridge, 2020) or Kohavi and Thomke, "The Surprising Power of Online Experiments" (HBR, 2017). No claims from them appear here.
- Neither paper names specific executives who sponsored or blocked experimentation at Bing.

## Fabijan, Dmitriev, Olsson, Bosch (ICSE 2017) and the Experimentation Evolution / Growth Model: stages and organisational changes

### Takeaway

The ICSE 2017 paper describes four phases (crawl, walk, run, fly) along technical, organisational and business dimensions; the organisational thread is a staged transfer of responsibility from a central data-science team to product-team "Experiment Owners", ending with teams that create, run, shut down and analyse experiments autonomously. Automatic small-percentage starts and auto-ramp appear at "run"; near-real-time detection and automatic emergency shutdown at "fly".

### Cited Findings

Source register for this section:

- **S3.** Aleksander Fabijan, Pavel Dmitriev, Helena Holmström Olsson, Jan Bosch, "The Evolution of Continuous Experimentation in Software Product Development: From Data to a Data-driven Organization at Scale," ICSE 2017 (May 20-28, 2017, Buenos Aires), pp. 770-780, DOI 10.1109/ICSE.2017.76 (page range and DOI from a search result, not from the PDF). [FULL-TEXT] of the author's version via PDF-to-text; Figure 5 (the model diagram) is an image I could not read. URL: <https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf>. Limits: a single-company inductive case study with a Microsoft-employed co-author; the paper itself says its contributions "risk being biased from this extensive inside view" and "can thus not directly translate to other companies". It is a prescriptive model ("we recommend"), not a measured history; it gives no dates for when each Microsoft team reached each phase.
- **S4.** Adrian Colyer, blog summary of S3, "The Morning Paper," 29 September 2017. [RELAYED]; secondary; used only to cross-check my reading. URL: <https://blog.acolyer.org/2017/09/29/the-evolution-of-continuous-experimentation-in-software-product-development/>
- **S5.** Tong Xia, Sumit Bhardwaj, Pavel Dmitriev, Aleksander Fabijan, "Safe Velocity: A Practical Guide to Software Deployment at Scale using Controlled Rollout," ICSE-SEIP 2019 (Montreal, May 2019). [FULL-TEXT] of the authors' version via PDF-to-text. URL: <https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf>. Limits: Office client apps, logs from "a several months long period of time in 2018"; the evaluation reports how often metric movements were detected, not how many incidents were prevented or what the false-positive rate was; no counterfactual.

Findings:

- Method and scope: collaboration with Microsoft's Analysis and Experimentation (A&E) team; historical data "collected over a period of two years" plus interviews; the first author was embedded for 10 weeks; partner teams were Bing, Cortana, Office, MSN.com, Skype and Xbox. The model was "inspired by a model developed internally at A&E". (S3 §III, §V) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- Teams were at very different maturities at the time of the study: in Office "data-driven development is" described as being in the early stages; MSN's team set up and ran experiments autonomously with embedded data scientists; at Bing, teams "conduct over 10.000 experiments per year ranging from large design modifications to every bug fix or minor improvement" and do so "without the help of the Analysis & Experimentation data scientists". (S3 §IV) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- Prerequisites: statistical skills (hypothesis testing, randomisation, sample size) and policies granting experimenters access to instrumentation data, which "in some domains ... is a serious concern" needing legal and technical handling. (S3 §V.A) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- **Crawl**: technical focus is logging/instrumentation (a centralised event catalogue) and a first experiment that can be run without a platform. Organisational: "experiment set-up, execution and analysis is conducted by a data scientist team"; product teams name an "Experiment Owner" as contact; a "standalone" data-science team trains product teams. Purpose of the first experiments is to gain traction and the "funding needed to develop an experimentation platform and culture". (S3 §V.B) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- **Walk**: platform adds power analysis and pre-experiment A/A; experiment count is low enough for "central planning and scheduling of experiments"; each experiment "is still closely monitored to detect user harm". Organisational: responsibility for creating experiments moves "from a data science expert to a product/program manager employed in the product team", but execution, monitoring and analysis stay with data scientists; data scientists become "embedded". The paper says bad practices "should be disputed (e.g. experimenting only on preview audience)". Business: the evaluation criterion becomes success metrics plus guardrail metrics plus data-quality metrics. (S3 §V.C) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- **Run** ("above 100 data-driven experiments per year"): platform adds alerting "together with the ability to divert traffic to control if an emergency situation occurs", carry-over control, and iteration support: experiments "should start on a small percentage of traffic (e.g. 0.5% of users assigned to treatment)" and the percentage "should automatically increase ... if no alerts on guardrail metrics were triggered". Stated reason: "as it gets easier to configure and start an experiment, the risk of user harm also increases". Organisational: Experiment Owners "receive the complete responsibility to execute their experiments", including "making shut-down and ramp-up decisions", while analysis is "still ... supervised by the data scientists"; a "partnership" model with data scientists employed in product teams and trained by the central team. (S3 §V.D) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- **Fly**: "controlled experiments are the norm for every change to any product in the company's portfolio", including "a minor bug fix". Platform adds interaction detection, "near real-time detection and automatic shutdown of harmful experiments" (in "run", alerting was computed periodically, "e.g. bi-hourly"), and a searchable institutional memory. Organisational: Experiment Owners "work autonomously. They create, execute and analyze"; the central team "reviews experiments only on demand". Business: the evaluation criterion is stable, is "used for setting the performance goals for teams", and changes "only periodically (e.g. once per year)". (S3 §V.E) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- Ramp-up as a harm limiter is described concretely for Bing: advanced features "prevent potentially harmful variants ... from affecting a large population by automatically checking alerts and incrementally ramping the number of users assigned to the treatment". (S3 §IV.D) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- The 2019 "Safe Velocity" paper combines rings with experiments ("controlled rollout", CRL) for Office. It criticises plain phased rollout on three grounds: it is slow, with "no natural notion of the 'optimal' time to keep the build in each ring, resulting in ad-hoc and highly variable procedures"; it "can only reliably detect large changes in metrics, e.g. 10% or larger"; and it watches reliability rather than whether users succeed. (S5 §II) — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)
- Office ring table (S5 Table 1): Dogfood (under a hundred to several hundred users; days to 1 week), Internal (hundreds to thousands; up to 1 week), Insiders (tens to hundreds of thousands; 2-4 weeks), Production (hundreds of thousands to millions; 2-4 weeks or longer). Early-ring pass criterion: no serious bug or crash moving guardrail metrics by over about 10%. — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)
- Evaluation numbers (S5 §V; Office client apps, 2018; p-value threshold 0.01): in Dogfood and Internal rings 35% of controlled rollouts showed significant guardrail movement, 79% of those within the first 3 days and the remaining 21% first on the 7-day scorecard; the authors predict only 3% would show a new signal at 14 days and conclude a 7-day period is the better choice. In Insiders and Production about 30% showed significant guardrail movement and 61% significant movement in OEC or local metrics, mostly within plus or minus 10%. — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)
- Automatic shutdown at Office: feature owners "can even require the system to automatically shut down the rollout if the effect is 'extremely harmful'". (S5 §IV.D, verbatim fragment) — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)
- Stated costs of introducing CRL (S5 §VI): dependence on telemetry and privacy compliance (GDPR); large-scale data systems; validating variant assignment "takes multiple months"; and the need to integrate data science into development teams, which "might seem to be slowing down the core tasks of the development team". — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)

### Inferences

- S3 is the cleanest source for "what managers must change" in experimentation: who is allowed to start, ramp and stop an experiment moves outward to the product team in steps, and the platform's automatic safeguards (small initial allocation, auto-ramp, auto-shutdown) are introduced before that authority is fully delegated. The ordering of safeguard-then-delegation is my reading of the phase descriptions, not a sentence in the paper.
- S5 complicates a "ramps are faster than soak" thesis. Microsoft's own guidance for Office is a minimum of 7 days per early ring and 2-4 weeks in later rings. What it claims over uncontrolled phased rollout is sensitivity and a principled duration, not short duration.
- S3 warns against "experimenting only on preview audience", and S5 notes ring populations are "not representative"; both are evidence that Microsoft regards internal/dogfood rings alone as an inadequate signal.

### Gaps

- The "Experimentation Growth Model" proper (Fabijan, Dmitriev, McFarland, Vermeer, Olsson, Bosch, "Experimentation growth: Evolving trustworthy A/B testing capabilities in online software companies," Journal of Software: Evolution and Process, 2018) was not retrieved. I cannot say how its dimensions differ from the 2017 model.
- S3 gives no dates for when Bing, MSN, Office or Xbox passed each phase, and no named managers.

## The 2014-2015 "combined engineering" reorganisation that removed the separate test discipline

### Takeaway

In the VSTS team the dev and test organisations were merged into one "engineering" organisation in roughly late 2014 to 2015, and Brian Harry states both the costs of the old model (developers not accountable, slow feedback) and its advantages (controllable test investment, a testing career path). First-person evidence is good for VSTS; for Windows and the company-wide 2014 layoffs I found only secondary reporting and could not open the best-known article (Ars Technica).

Findings:

- What the old model was (VSTS): "Two years ago, we had 10's of thousands of tests. They were written by 'testers' to test code written by 'developers'." (S6, verbatim, 20 words) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)

Disadvantages: "lack of accountability on the developers, slow feedback cycle", little motivation to make code testable, and test architecture diverging from code architecture. (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Cost of the old test estate: "full testing would take the better part of a day to run", plus hours to analyse false failures and "days or weeks to repair" tests broken by legitimate product changes; most tests were end-to-end UI or command-line automation. (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/). Bekker's report of Hodges gives "as of September 2014, nightly tests took 22 hours and the full run took two days" (S10, relayed) — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx)
- The change, in the decision-maker's words: "We combined the dev and test orgs into a consolidating 'engineering' org. For the most part, we eliminated the distinction between people who code and people who test." (S6, two consecutive sentences; second is 16 words) He qualifies: not everyone does identical amounts, "but every person does some of everything and is accountable for the quality of what they produce". — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Why: test was "one of our biggest remaining impediments to our goal of 'continuous delivery'" and the problem was "everything about test: our org, roles, frameworks, tests, harnesses, analysis". Simply speeding up existing processes "would never get us to where we wanted to be". (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- When: Harry, writing June 2017, says "a little over 2 years ago", implying roughly spring 2015 (S6). Bekker reports Hodges as saying the roles were merged in November 2014 (S10, relayed). These differ by about half a year; the org merge may have preceded the test rewrite. — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/); [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx)
- The test estate was thrown away, not ported: they "set out to completely throw away our 10's of thousands of tests that took 8 years to create". The Learn page quantifies it: 27,000 legacy ("TRA") tests at sprint 78, 14,000 by sprint 101, zero at sprint 120, "42 sprints over 126 weeks"; "many were simply deleted, based on team analysis of their usefulness". (S6; S7 case study) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/); [Microsoft Learn, Shift testing left](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable)
- The cost and the leader's impatience: "this effort to completely redo our test system over the past 2+years has been a massive investment"; in some sprints "it was most of what a feature team did"; and "18 months into it and lots of missed opportunity cost later, I started to agitate about when we were going to be 'done'" (verbatim, 21 words). He judges it worth it but offers no figures: "I'd hate to even try to calculate the total cost". (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/). The Learn page calls it "a non-negotiable requirement for the team's quality and performance goals" (S7) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable)
- Testing change and ramping are stated to be inseparable: "We couldn't really rely on Test in Production the way we are headed without also doing ring based deployments". (S6, verbatim, 17 words) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Ownership rule after the change: "push accountability for testing to the person writing the component code. Don't rely on others to test the component." (S7, "Make code owners responsible for testing") — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable)
- Resistance acknowledged: "many product developers didn't learn to write unit tests"; there was "skepticism about whether a unit test strategy works" and "fear that unit tests will replace functional tests". The team began by leaving old functional tests alone and making new unit tests easy, to "build momentum". (S7) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable)
- Discipline structure before and after, per Learn's table: "PM, Dev, and Test" became "PM, Design, and Engineering"; "Engineering builds the products and ensures their quality". Teams are 10-12 people, "self-managing", and "own feature deployment" and "own features in production". (S8) — [Microsoft Learn, How Microsoft plans](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/plan/how-microsoft-plans-devops)
- A Microsoft employee's account of the rationale: "We wanted to reduce delays in handoffs between developers and testers and focus on quality for all software created"; "This does not mean testing was abandoned, quite the contrary." Operations also moved into the same organisation as "service engineers", and change management shifted "from Ops to Dev". (S9, relayed quotations) — [Almeida 2016](https://www.infoq.com/articles/devops-lessons-microsoft)
- A senior tester's contemporaneous view: "Combined engineering doesn't mean that everyone on the team does the exact same thing"; "every successful combined engineering team I've seen has had plenty of people who were doing testing activities"; and a warning: "The future gets scary, however, when organizations think that code quality is the same as product quality." (S11, relayed quotations, Dec 2014) — [Page 2014](https://www.a1qa.com/blog/test-specialists-are-essential-members-of-software-teams-interview-with-alan-page/)
- Complicating recollection (unverified, pseudonymous): the author says that in practice "all the testers were fired or turned into developers", that velocity "hadn't dropped", that ex-testers did roughly 70% testing work, that "a large number of testing is outsourced", and that by 2021 Microsoft was hiring for pure testing roles again. (S12, relayed) — [Quality Weekly 2021](https://qaweekly.substack.com/p/how-microsoft-went-from-test-to-no)
- Company-level context, from search snippets only [SNIPPET]: press in July-October 2014 reported that the 18,000-job reduction announced by Satya Nadella fell heavily on test engineers, especially in Windows, and that developers and program managers would absorb testing duties. I could not open the underlying articles (Bloomberg, Business Insider, ZDNet/Mary Jo Foley), so I give no quotations and no link beyond the search listing — [eWeek listing](https://www.eweek.com/it-management/microsoft-bracing-for-job-cuts/)

### Inferences

- The VSTS evidence shows removal of the test discipline as one move in a bundle: merge orgs, make the author accountable, rewrite the test pyramid over two-plus years, add rings and flags. Harry's own sentence ties test-in-production to rings. None of the sources treats "delete the QA gate" as sufficient or safe on its own.
- The merge coincided with a company-wide layoff. The primary sources read here frame it as an engineering decision about accountability and feedback speed; the secondary reporting frames it partly as headcount reduction. I have no primary source that settles the relative weight of the two motives.
- For a finance audience: Harry lists "clearly measurable and controllable investment in test" as a real advantage that was given up. A regulated shop that needs demonstrable, separable test evidence is an example of exactly that advantage.

### Gaps

- Munil Shah's talks ("shift left / shift right") were not found in a readable form; a Channel 9 interview exists but is video and was not watched. No statement is attributed to him.
- Sam Guckenheimer: the InfoQ interview (Shane Hastie, 17 Oct 2014) read via the fetch tool covers cloud adoption and does not discuss testers, flags or canaries; the December 2015 devblogs post is only a pointer to a video and says the division went "from shipping a box product ~every year to delivering value every week" [RELAYED] — [devblogs 2015](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/devops/developer-divisions-transformation-to-devops-by-sam-guckenheimer/). His e-book "Our Journey to Cloud Cadence" was not retrieved.
- Bing's own combined-engineering change (often said to predate 2014) is not documented in anything I read; S2 shows Bing still had "testers" in 2013 and nothing more.
- No source gives numbers of testers converted, departed, or laid off in any team.

## Azure DevOps / VSTS: rings ("safe deployment"), feature flags, shift-right testing, and Brian Harry's and Buck Hodges's retrospectives

### Takeaway

The VSTS team's rings and staged flag exposure were built in reaction to specific outages in late 2013, when a single-instance service, a batch of flags flipped on the morning of a launch, and no canary produced days of degradation that turning flags back off did not cure. The mature practice still looks like a release train in some respects: a three-week sprint deployment, about 24 hours of bake per ring, and human approval on the first ring.

Findings on cadence:

- The first three-week sprint was in August 2010 and November 2017 was sprint 127 (Hodges via Bekker, S10, relayed). Harry in June 2017 refers to "the intervening 6 years or so" of transformations in "how we plan, track progress, deploy, manage feedback, monitor, architect, develop and test" and says "If we'd tried to do it all at once. I'm sure we would have failed." (S6) — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx); [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Learn's before/after table: "4-6 month milestones" became "3-week sprints"; "Features ship once a year" became "Features ship every sprint"; "Feature branches" became "Everyone works in the main branch". (S8) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/plan/how-microsoft-plans-devops)
- Deployment is batched, deliberately not continuous: teams "batch up deployments into timed releases, usually aligned with a three-week sprint cadence", because deploying each of 200+ daily pull requests across data centres for test before merge would leave developers "waiting for branches to merge". A release branch (e.g. `releases/M129`) is cut each sprint and rolled through rings; during rollout "there are actually two branches in production". (S21) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/how-microsoft-develops-devops)

Findings on the 2013-2014 outages that drove rings:

- November 2013 (Visual Studio 2013 launch): "I feel terrible about it and I can't apologize enough." The incident ran about a week and a half. On launch morning the team enabled flags for branding and billing, including one enforcing terms-of-service acceptance. (S14) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- Flags did not save them: they rolled back "all of the feature switches that we enabled that morning ... Unfortunately it didn't. And no one is completely sure why." Harry adds the flags had "been changed enough times that it's hard to tell for certain" whether the rollback was done correctly. (S14) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/). Hodges, per Bekker: "we started turning feature flags off and it wouldn't recover" (S10, relayed); Bekker reports about two dozen features were flipped an hour before the keynote and recovery took about two weeks — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx)
- Harry's diagnosis of the structural gap: "Aside from our ability to control with feature flags (which is very nice) all customers are affected or none." (verbatim, 20 words) and "The service is now at a scale where there's just too much risk in that." He wants to "roll out changes to smaller groups of people and observe the behavior in a production environment". (S14) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- Harry does not abandon pre-production testing: "We need to revisit our pre-production load testing. Some of these issues should have been caught before they went to production." He calls it "a delicate balance because 'there's no place like production' but 'trust thy neighbor but lock your door.'" (S14) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- A people-management lesson (advice from Scott Guthrie, per Harry): after a set number of hours on an incident, "send half the team home to sleep" so fresh people can rotate in; Harry did this at about 8 pm on launch day. (S14) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- February 2014: "since ~Oct, we've had a run of 'bad' deployments that caused unacceptable down time"; "the scale of the service had outgrown the engineering processes and resiliency we had built in". (S15) — [Harry 2014a](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/visual-studio-online-update-feb-10/)
- The first response was to shift deployments to weekends. It reduced customer impact but "it's clear weekend deployments aren't really going to be sustainable"; sprint 59's weekend deployment had people "working all day Saturday and Sunday". "Time shifting the deployment isn't a fix, it's a mitigation". The stated goal is that "every deployment is a 'non-event'". (S15) — [Harry 2014a](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/visual-studio-online-update-feb-10/)
- Birth of ring 0 (deployed about late January 2014): an additional instance "affectionately called 'ring 0'" (Scale Unit 0, San Antonio) "will be where all production deployments happen first", holding only accounts that "volunteer" for extra volatility, including Harry's own; "Once deployments are validated on ring 0, they will incrementally roll out across other instances." (S15) — [Harry 2014a](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/visual-studio-online-update-feb-10/)
- Harry withholds credit: "I'd like to see 3 or 4 smooth ones in a row before I'm willing to give too much credit." (S15, verbatim, 20 words) — [Harry 2014a](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/visual-studio-online-update-feb-10/)
- November 2014 (7.5-hour outage triggered by a SQL Azure change): "according to standard practice, the new behavior was coded behind a feature flag. So, once we identified the cause, it was easy to mitigate". But "it took approximately 45 minutes to figure out how to turn it off due to tooling issues and lack of documentation", and "66 minutes to get ahold of an engineer that understood the malfunctioning code" against an expectation of 15. (S16) — [Harry 2014b](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/outage-on-monday-nov-24)
- Harry's conclusions there: "there's no way to stop all bugs from getting through"; "We need to roll out new changes in a more controlled fashion."; "Being down 30% is better than being down 100%."; and "I've been suggesting for a couple of years now that we have an Azure wide canary instance." Each service had its own canary, so no single place tested all changes against all services. (S16) — [Harry 2014b](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/outage-on-monday-nov-24)

Findings on feature flags (practice as of 2016):

- Goals: "Our first goal is decoupling deployment and exposure."; scope control from global to scale unit to account to user; early feedback; "turn it off quickly". "At the core, a feature flag is nothing more than an input to an if statement in the code". (S17) — [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/)
- Exposure stages: Stage 0 "Canary" (the VSTS team's account plus select internal accounts), Stage 1 (MVPs and opted-in top customers), Stage 2 (private preview), Stage 3 (public preview), Stage 4 (general availability). "Program managers are responsible for sending out communication to the users once the feature flags are enabled." Speed through stages "depends on the scope of the feature, feedback, and telemetry". (S17) — [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/)
- Two axes, not one: "tiers are for deployments, and stages are for feature flags". (S19) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/progressive-experimentation-feature-flags)
- Test burden doubles: "We need to test both with the feature flag on and off to ensure it works. This also critical for ensuring the feature can be turned off quickly". (S17) — [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/)
- Flag debt: "It's easy to accumulate a lot of flags that are no longer used"; teams delete the flag and old path "a few weeks or a few months later". One XML file per service creates "a natural motivation to control the size of that file"; Learn recommends a flag review "at the beginning of each sprint", and warns that a retained flag's old path "needs to be tested and maintained until the option is removed". (S17; S19) — [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/progressive-experimentation-feature-flags)
- The rule learned from 2013: "We learned the hard way that it's not the morning of the event." Turning on many flags at once meant "a large amount of new code interacting with the system under production load, and the system fell apart"; "At that time, we didn't have stages, and we only had one public scale unit." Now flags go on "at least 24 hours ahead of an event". (S17) — [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/). Bekker adds that features are now enabled one at a time (S10, relayed) — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx)
- Who decides: "The feature teams own the decision as to whether they need a feature flag or not for a given change. Not every change requires one". (S19) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/progressive-experimentation-feature-flags)
- Telemetry caveat: avoid "the trap of assuming everything is okay just because nothing bad seems to be happening". (S19) Hodges, per Bekker: a feature with no failures may simply have low traffic (S10, relayed). — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/progressive-experimentation-feature-flags)
- Platform advice: Learn now recommends buying a flag platform rather than building one. (S19) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/progressive-experimentation-feature-flags)

Findings on rings / safe deployment:

- Example tier model (S18): Tier 0 internal only, "high tolerance for risk and bugs", which "finds most of the user-impacting bugs introduced by the deployment"; Tier 1 a small data centre with customers using a breadth of the product; Tier 2 a medium or large data centre for scale issues; Tier 3 large internal accounts and European customers; Tier 4 everyone else. — [Microsoft Learn, Safe deployment practices](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)
- Bake time: "a 24-hour day should be enough time for most scenarios to expose latent bugs", and it should include a peak-usage period, "requiring a full business day" for services that peak in business hours. (S18) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)
- Hotfix rules: a Sev 0 fix "may be deployed directly to the impacted scale unit"; Sev 1 "must be deployed through tier 0"; lower severities "must be deployed through all tiers as planned". (S18) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)
- Approval: as of 2016, VSTS used four deployment rings across 12 scale units; "lead engineers approved the first ring, and the rest was automated"; the team's own account was in the first scale unit: "If anything is going to break, they want it to break on themselves first." (S9, relayed) — [Almeida 2016](https://www.infoq.com/articles/devops-lessons-microsoft)
- Deploy in working hours: "it becomes advantageous to push deployments during working hours, especially early in the day and early in the week", so problems surface while "everyone will already be working". (S18) This reverses the weekend-deployment mitigation of early 2014 (S15). — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)
- The mindset named as the obstacle: "Teams used to deploying once every few months have a mindset that buffers for stabilization." Listed early problems: payloads too big, no service isolation, manual steps, "Only Ops can do deployments", and bad deployments that cannot be rolled back. (S18) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)
- Quality gates remain: the page opens by saying problems occur "despite using best practices and passing all quality gates", and warns teams against writing tests "simply to change a yellow warning to a green approval". (S18) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)

Findings on shift-right / test in production:

- The argument: "Deploying to a QA or staging service can simulate a comparable environment, but there's no full substitute for the production environment." Production offers real diversity, real workload, and keeps changing even when the app does not. (S20) — [Microsoft Learn, Shift right](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/deliver/shift-right-test-production)
- An explicit cost-benefit condition: it is better to catch a purchase-blocking bug "when less than 1% of customers are on that deployment tier", but "The feature value with detected failures must exceed the net losses of those failures, measured in a meaningful way for the given business." (S20) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/deliver/shift-right-test-production)
- A limit on chaos testing: it "should be limited to canary environments that have little or no customer impact". (S20) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/deliver/shift-right-test-production)
- Taxonomy linking left and right: L0/L1 unit tests on every pull request, L2 functional tests against a deployment with stubs, L3 tests against production. Harry in 2017: "We haven't established a consistent practice for running L3's". The Learn page's later numbering adds an L4 for production tests, and says "deployment might be blocked if L4 tests fail". (S6; S7) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable)

Findings on on-call and live-site ownership:

- Designated responsible individuals (DRIs) were formalised in October 2013; on call 24/7 in 12-hour shifts rotated weekly, with response targets of five minutes by day and 15 at night; each roughly 10-person feature team rotates two members into a "shield team" for live-site work (Hodges via Bekker, S10, relayed). Learn describes the same idea as F-crew (features) and C-crew (customer/live site), replacing an earlier "interrupt culture". (S8) — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/plan/how-microsoft-plans-devops)
- Alert hygiene as a prerequisite: Hodges, per Bekker: "We were drowning in alerts"; rules now require every alert to be actionable (S10, relayed). — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx)
- Manager-enforced quality rule: a bug cap of five bugs per engineer; a team over the cap at sprint end "must stop working on new features and fix bugs". (S8; also S7) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/plan/how-microsoft-plans-devops)
- Scorecard managers track: live-site time to detect, time to mitigate, count and age of repair items from incident retrospectives; engineering bug debt; pipeline velocity. The plan page lists "Number of bugs found" and "Code coverage" among metrics teams avoid for tracking progress toward objectives. (S7; S8) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/plan/how-microsoft-plans-devops)
- Architecture investment alongside: the service was split from one global service into 31 services, with circuit breakers borrowed from Netflix (Hodges via Bekker, S10, relayed). — [Bekker 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx)

### Inferences

- The sequence in the primary sources is: cloud cadence first (2010), then DRIs (Oct 2013), then painful outages (Oct 2013 to early 2014), then ring 0 (early 2014), then the dev/test merge and test rewrite (late 2014 to 2017), then documented stages for flags (by 2016). Rings and stages were remedies adopted after flags alone, on a single instance, had failed in public. The ordering is assembled by me from dated posts.
- The VSTS model is itself a release train with environment promotion: a three-week batch, a release branch, promotion through five tiers with roughly a day of bake each. The difference from the essay audience's "three days of promotion and soak" is that the promotion environments are slices of production carrying real traffic, and feature exposure is separated from deployment. An essay that presents Microsoft as having eliminated soak would misstate these sources.
- Harry's own posts support two cautions: flags can fail as a rollback mechanism (2013), and a kill switch is only as fast as the tooling and documentation around it (2014: 45 minutes to find how to flip it).
- The reversal from weekend deployments (2014) to working-hours deployments (later Learn guidance) is a clean example of risk perception changing once blast radius was controllable.

### Gaps

- No dated primary source was found for when the five-tier model and the 24-hour bake were adopted; the Learn pages carry only 2022 revision dates.
- The MSDN Magazine article on ring-based deployment and the "phase-rollout-with-rings" doc linked from S21 were not read.
- I found no statement of how VSTS decides to halt a ring progression (automated health gate versus human judgement) beyond "lead engineers approved the first ring" in S9.
- Ed Blankenship's talks were not located.

## Measured outcomes Microsoft reported, and stated limits or regrets

### Takeaway

Microsoft's public sources give cycle-time and test-system numbers and detection-rate numbers, but I found no published before/after measurement of production quality (incident counts, availability, escaped defects) attributable to the dev/test merge, rings or flags. The limits and regrets are, by contrast, stated candidly by the implementers.

### Cited Findings

Measured or quantified outcomes:

- Test cycle: from a full run taking "the better part of a day" (S6) to about 60,000 unit tests in under six minutes; pull request to merge in about 30 minutes; and "Within two hours from Merge to SelfHost, the entire product is tested and the change is ready to go into production." (S7) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/shift-left-make-testing-fast-reliable)
- Flaky-test system: rolled out in sprint 116; from sprints 116 to 119, 170 reliability bugs filed with average resolution 3.75 days; some turned out to be product race conditions (S6, relayed for these figures; I verified the surrounding text but did not re-check these two numbers on the raw page) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Volume: a product with 200+ pull requests a day producing 300+ CI builds and 500+ test runs every 24 hours. (S21) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/develop/how-microsoft-develops-devops)
- Release cadence: 4-6 month milestones and yearly feature shipping to three-week sprints with features shipping every sprint. (S8) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/plan/how-microsoft-plans-devops)
- Harry's qualitative outcome claim: "we are starting to reap the benefits in improved quality, agility and engineer satisfaction". No numbers accompany it. (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Experimentation scale: two experiments in ExP's first year (2006-07) and eight in the second (S1); over 200 concurrent at Bing by 2013 (S2); over 10,000 a year at Bing by the 2017 paper (S3). — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf); [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf); [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)
- Value claims: the Bing system "is credited with having accelerated innovation and increased annual revenues by hundreds of millions of dollars", and identified "many negative features that we avoided deploying, despite key stakeholders' early excitement". Authors' own claim. (S2 abstract) — [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Office controlled rollouts (2018): 35% of early-ring rollouts showed significant guardrail movement, 79% of those within 3 days; about 30% of later-ring rollouts showed guardrail movement. This measures detection, not prevented harm. (S5 §V) — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)
- A Bing bot-detection experiment showed about 10% infrastructure saving "without introducing user harm". (S3 §IV.D) — [Fabijan et al. 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)

Stated limits, costs and regrets:

- The separate-test model had real advantages that were surrendered: measurable, controllable test investment and a testing career path. (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- The test rewrite cost more than two years, sometimes most of a team's sprint, with "lots of missed opportunity cost". (S6) — [Harry 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)
- Flags without rings were insufficient and flag rollback failed to restore service in November 2013. (S14; S17) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/); [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/)
- Pre-production testing is still required: "Some of these issues should have been caught before they went to production." (S14) — [Harry 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)
- Kill switches depend on tooling: 45 minutes to work out how to turn a flag off; 66 minutes to reach an engineer who knew the code. (S16) — [Harry 2014b](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/outage-on-monday-nov-24)
- Flags add permanent test and maintenance cost while they live (both states must be tested; dead flags accumulate). (S17; S19) — [Hodges 2016](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/buckh/controlling-exposure-through-feature-flags-in-vs-team-services/)
- Production testing has an explicit business condition (value must exceed the net loss from failures) and chaos work is confined to canaries. (S20) — [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/deliver/shift-right-test-production)
- Experimentation limits: not for strategy formulation; needs an agreed metric; multiple code paths are expensive; incentives were not demonstrably changed by 2009; alerting is useless if naive; experiments typically need two weeks. (S1; S2) — [Kohavi et al. 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf); [Kohavi et al. 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)
- Ring rollouts for Office need at least 7 days in early rings and 2-4 weeks later; ring populations are unrepresentative; uncontrolled phased rollout only reliably detects changes of about 10% or more. (S5) — [Xia et al. 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)
- A practitioner's caution that developer-owned code quality is not the same as product quality. (S11, relayed) — [Page 2014](https://www.a1qa.com/blog/test-specialists-are-essential-members-of-software-teams-interview-with-alan-page/)

### Inferences

- What leaders changed, assembled across sources (each element is cited above; the list is my synthesis):
  1. **Ownership**: the author of code became accountable for its tests and its behaviour in production (S6, S7, S8); feature teams "own features in production".
  2. **QA/test roles**: the separate discipline was merged into engineering in VSTS; testing skill was meant to persist inside teams (S6, S9, S11), and the legacy test suite was replaced, not inherited.
  3. **Approval**: change management moved from Ops to Dev (S9); a lead engineer approves ring 0 and later rings are automated (S9); experiment owners take over ramp-up and shut-down decisions in stages (S3); feature teams decide whether a change needs a flag (S19).
  4. **Incentives**: bug cap that halts feature work (S8); scorecards on time-to-detect, time-to-mitigate and repair items (S7); proposal, not proven practice, to tie goals to metrics rather than shipping (S1); OEC used to set team goals at the "fly" stage (S3).
  5. **Risk perception**: from "buffer for stabilization" and weekend deployments to small payloads, working-hours deployments and "break on ourselves first" (S15, S18, S9); from "do it right the first time" to "admit that mistakes will happen" and detect them (S1, S2).
  6. **On-call**: DRIs from October 2013, shield team / C-crew rotation, send-half-home rule in long incidents, actionable-alert rule (S10, S8, S14).
  7. **Platform investment**: central experimentation platform and class (S1, S2, S3); multi-instance scale units and ring 0 (S15); flag framework with stages (S17); two-plus years of test-system rewrite (S6); service decomposition and circuit breakers (S10); telemetry.
- The record supports "Microsoft replaced a days-long pre-release test pass and yearly releases with fast pre-merge tests plus progressive production exposure". It does not support "Microsoft showed that removing soak improves quality"; no such measurement was published in anything I read.
- Every success story here is for a product where a bad change degrades service for a subset of users and can be reversed. None of these sources addresses changes whose first execution is irreversible, which is the obvious objection a finance engineer will raise.

### Gaps

- No published availability, incident-rate or escaped-defect trend for VSTS before and after rings or the test merge was found. Harry's posts describe individual incidents, not a trend.
- No Microsoft source quantifies the cost of the experimentation platform or of the flag/ring infrastructure.
- Windows and Azure core-platform "safe deployment practices" (as distinct from Azure DevOps) were not researched; Azure's own SDP blog posts by Mark Russinovich and colleagues exist but were not read.
- Video talks (Munil Shah, Sam Guckenheimer, Buck Hodges, the "tiers and stages" video linked from S19) were not watched; nothing is attributed to them except via the written reports identified above.
