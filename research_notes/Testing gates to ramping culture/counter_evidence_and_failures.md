## Knight Capital, 1 August 2012: what the SEC order actually establishes

### Takeaway

The SEC order describes a repurposed flag that re-activated dormant, never-retested code on one of eight servers that a technician missed, with no second-person review, no written deployment procedure, ignored pre-open alerts, no automated position/kill control, and a rollback that made things worse. It is simultaneously the canonical flag-reuse disaster and a case where the code deployment *was* staged over several days but exposure was all-at-once, so it cuts against flags-as-practised-carelessly and against "staged deployment" that is not staged *exposure*; it does not show that longer pre-release soak would have helped.

### Cited Findings

Source: U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, "In the Matter of Knight Capital Americas LLC", Securities Exchange Act Release No. 70694, Administrative Proceeding File No. 3-15570, 16 October 2013. **[FULL-TEXT]**, paragraphs 1-43 read; later paragraphs (undertakings) skimmed. Settled order: Knight consented "without admitting or denying the findings". — [SEC order](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)

- Scale (¶1, ¶17): while processing 212 small retail parent orders, SMARS sent millions of child orders over about 45 minutes, obtaining "over 4 million executions in 154 stocks for more than 397 million shares"; net long about $3.5 billion in 80 stocks, net short about $3.15 billion in 74 stocks; loss "over $460 million". — [SEC order ¶1, ¶17](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Market impact (¶18): for 75 stocks Knight's executions were more than 20% of volume and contributed to price moves above 5%; for 37 of those, price moved more than 10% and Knight was more than 50% of volume. — [SEC order ¶18](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Knight's size (¶11): about ten percent of all trading in listed U.S. equity securities; SMARS about one percent or more. — [SEC order ¶11](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- The change (¶12): new code in SMARS for the NYSE Retail Liquidity Program (RLP), scheduled to begin 1 August 2012. — [SEC order ¶12](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- The flag and dead code (¶13): the new RLP code was intended to replace unused "Power Peg" code; that functionality "remained present and callable"; the new code "repurposed a flag that was formerly used to activate the Power Peg code." Knight intended to delete Power Peg so the flag would engage RLP instead. — [SEC order ¶13](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Dormancy and the untested refactor (¶14): Knight ceased using Power Peg in 2003; in 2005 it moved the cumulative-quantity tracking function to an earlier point in the SMARS code sequence and "did not retest the Power Peg code after moving the cumulative quantity function". (So the dormant code was about nine years unused and had been silently broken for about seven.) — [SEC order ¶14](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Deployment (¶15): "Beginning on July 27, 2012, Knight deployed the new RLP code in SMARS in stages by placing it on a limited number of servers in SMARS on successive days." One technician "did not copy the new code to one of the eight SMARS computer servers." "Knight did not have a second technician review this deployment". "Knight had no written procedures that required such a review." — [SEC order ¶15](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Failure mechanism (¶16): seven servers processed RLP orders correctly; orders with the repurposed flag reaching the eighth server triggered the defective Power Peg code, which sent child orders continuously "without regard to the number of share executions Knight had already received". — [SEC order ¶16](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- The 97 e-mails (¶19): from about 8:01 a.m. ET an internal system generated automated "BNET rejects" referencing SMARS with the error "Power Peg disabled"; 97 were sent to a group of Knight personnel before the 9:30 open. "Knight did not design these types of messages to be system alerts, and Knight personnel generally did not review them". The order calls them a "potential opportunity to identify and fix the coding issue prior to the market open." These pre-market orders were distinct from the 212 parent orders (fn. 6). — [SEC order ¶19](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- No halt/kill control (¶21): Knight had no control comparing orders leaving SMARS with those entering it and "did not have procedures in place to halt SMARS's operations in response to its own aberrant activity." The one price-collar control (9.5% from NBBO) did not apply to orders received pre-open for the opening auction, such as the 212. The order does not use the phrase "kill switch". — [SEC order ¶21](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- No automated capital threshold (¶22-25): the "33 Account" had a $2 million gross position limit not linked to any automated control; PMON, the primary risk tool, was post-execution, "relied entirely on human monitoring", did not display limits, and lagged under volume. Footnote 7: Knight *did* have automatic shutdown on P&L limits for certain strategies of one trading group. — [SEC order ¶22-25](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Code development and deployment controls (¶26): no written code development and deployment procedures for SMARS "(although other groups at Knight had written procedures)"; no second-technician review; no written protocol on accessing unused code on production servers, such as requiring retest. — [SEC order ¶26](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Incident response and the fix that made it worse (¶27): no supervisory incident-response procedures; reliance on the technology team "in a live trading environment". "Knight uninstalled the new RLP code from the seven servers where it had been deployed correctly. This action worsened the problem", activating Power Peg on those servers too. — [SEC order ¶27](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Prior warning (¶33): in October 2011 Knight used test data in a weekend disaster-recovery test; the LMM desk kept using the test data on Monday, losing nearly $7.5 million. Knight fixed the specific cause but "did not broadly consider whether it had sufficient controls to prevent the entry of erroneous orders". — [SEC order ¶33](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Management/compliance (¶28-32, ¶34): the initial 15c3-5 assessment inventoried existing controls and "did not consider possible problems within SMARS"; post-compliance reviews did not consider controls "concerning code deployment or unused code residing on servers"; the CEO's March 2012 certification said Knight had "processes" to comply rather than certifying compliance (described as an unintentional drafting error). — [SEC order ¶28-34](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- What the SEC says would have prevented it (¶41): "a written procedure requiring a simple double-check of the deployment of the RLP code could have identified that a server had been missed"; integrating BNET rejects into monitoring "likewise could have prevented the events"; a written protocol requiring retest of Power Peg in 2005 could have found the disabled function. ¶42: Knight "needed clear guidance for its technology personnel as to when to disconnect a malfunctioning system from the market." — [SEC order ¶41-42](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- SEC's general statement on technology risk (¶4): prudent technology risk management "has, at its core, quality assurance, continuous improvement, controlled testing and user acceptance, process measuring, management and control". — [SEC order ¶4](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Violation and penalty: Exchange Act Section 15(c)(3) and Rule 15c3-5 (the Market Access Rule), specifically 15c3-5(b), (c)(1)(i), (c)(1)(ii), (e)(1), (e)(2) (¶9 A-F, ¶36-43); collateral Regulation SHO Rules 200(g) and 203(b) (¶10, ¶35). Civil money penalty $12,000,000; also retention of an independent consultant (Section IV of the order). — [SEC order ¶9, ¶36-43, Section IV](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)

Secondary retelling to handle with care: Doug Seven, "Knightmare: A DevOps Cautionary Tale", 17 April 2014, personal blog. **[FETCH]**. Seven states "This is my telling of the story based on what I have read (I was not involved in this)" and cites Release No. 70694. — [Doug Seven blog](https://dougseven.com/2014/04/17/knightmare-a-devops-cautionary-tale/)

- Details in Seven's post that are NOT in the SEC order (as far as the paragraphs I read show): that the new code was "thoroughly tested" (the order is silent on testing of the RLP code; it only says Power Peg was not retested); "8-years" of dead code (the order says use ceased in 2003, about nine years before); about 17% market share on NYSE/NASDAQ (order ¶11 says about ten percent of listed U.S. equity trading); $365 million cash on hand; the 48-hour, roughly $400 million rescue; the phrase "no kill switch"; "by 9:31 AM" Wall Street recognition; and the explicit claim that an automated deployment system would have avoided the error. — [Doug Seven blog](https://dougseven.com/2014/04/17/knightmare-a-devops-cautionary-tale/); compare [SEC order](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- The order never uses the word "manual", but ¶15's "did not copy the new code" supports describing the deployment as manual. The remedy the SEC names is a written double-check procedure, not automation (¶41). — [SEC order ¶15, ¶41](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Another inaccurate academic retelling: Rahman et al. (MSR 2016, §7 discussion of dead code) describe Knight as a group "which produces software for trading companies" and quote Jim Bird's account of the reused toggle. Knight was a broker-dealer/market maker (order ¶11). — [Rahman et al. 2016](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~pcr/paper/Rahman2016MSR.pdf)

Cuts: **Against naive flag practice** (flag reuse, dead code behind a flag, version skew across servers, rollback of code while flag state stays live). **For** the essay's process argument that automated, verified, uniform deployment and automated kill/guardrail controls matter more than calendar time. **Neutral-to-against soak**: nothing in the order says more soak time would have caught it; the code sat on servers from 27 July to 1 August without incident because the path was dormant until RLP launched.

### Inferences

- The deployment was "in stages ... on successive days" (¶15) but the *activation* was a single external event (RLP go-live, ¶12, ¶16). This is the precise distinction the essay needs: staged deployment of dormant code is not a ramp. Five days of "bake" on production servers observed nothing because nothing exercised the path. The same pattern recurs in Google Cloud June 2025 (below).
- A percentage ramp of the flag would only have helped if (a) flag evaluation were centrally controlled rather than carried on inbound orders, and (b) an automated guardrail watched order-out versus order-in or position. The order's ¶21-25 show neither existed. Flags without automated guardrail metrics and a tested disconnect are not a safety mechanism.
- "Roll back" is not automatically safe: ¶27's rollback re-created the fault on seven more servers because code and flag state were versioned independently. This directly challenges any essay claim that flags make rollback trivially safe.
- Management reading: the SEC's findings are about governance (no written procedures, reviews that inventoried controls instead of asking what could go wrong, a narrow response to the October 2011 loss). Finance readers will read Knight as "controls and procedures", which is closer to their existing release-train instincts than to "move fast".

### Gaps

- The order does not say how the RLP code itself was tested, whether a test environment replicated eight servers, or how the flag was carried (it says "orders sent with the repurposed flag", ¶16). Claims about test coverage of the new code cannot be sourced to the order.
- The order does not state the number of child orders precisely ("millions").
- I did not read the undertakings section in detail beyond the penalty amount and consultant requirement.

## Other finance release failures with primary documentation

### Takeaway

The regulator record is mixed and should be reported as such: TSB (2018) and Nasdaq/Facebook (2012) are failures of a single large event with testing that was reduced or unrepresentative, which supports both "smaller staged changes" and "more/better pre-release testing"; RBS (2012) is an untested back-out, which undermines the assumption that rollback is a safe default. Several are one-shot events (an IPO cross, a platform cutover with no rollback) where a ramp is hard or impossible by construction.

### Cited Findings

**TSB migration, April 2018.** Financial Conduct Authority, Final Notice to TSB Bank plc, 20 December 2022. **[FULL-TEXT]** for the paragraphs cited; the notice is long (about 5,600 extracted lines) and I read the summary section and selected section 4 paragraphs, not all of it. — [FCA Final Notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)

- Penalty: FCA £29.75 million (after 30% settlement discount; £42.50 million before) (¶1.1). The parallel PRA penalty is in a separate PRA notice I did not read. — [FCA Final Notice ¶1.1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- The change (¶2.5): over 20-22 April 2018 TSB ran the "Main Migration Event", migrating "the majority of the operations of its corporate systems, customer services and customer data" to the new Proteo4UK platform; serious issues began shortly after go-live. — [FCA Final Notice ¶2.5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- Big-bang by choice (¶4.48-4.49): the parent's existing methodology was "a 'big bang' or single operation data migration approach"; TSB "opted for a predominantly single event data migration", with some functionality moved earlier in "Governed Transition Events" (Faster Payments, mobile app, ATMs). Stated reasons included the difficulty and cost of a staged migration between two providers' platforms and a belief that Lloyds was resistant to a staged approach. — [FCA Final Notice ¶4.48-4.49](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- There was a limited live pilot: the plan included a "Friends & Family proving pilot" operating "a fully functional live version of the new platform for a pilot set" of customers, later extended as "TSB Beta" (section 4 programme-plan paragraphs). — [FCA Final Notice, section 4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- Testing compressed (¶2.13): test phases meant to run sequentially "ended up running in parallel, or being changed in scope. Testing only concluded the day before the decision to migrate was taken." — [FCA Final Notice ¶2.13](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- The specific missed test (¶2.14): a decision "outside of the appropriate governance forum" around end-February 2018 reduced non-functional testing of digital channels in "Active-Active configuration" across the data centres. "Had this testing been conducted, it is likely that a problem in the configuration of certain components in the data centres ... would have been found." — [FCA Final Notice ¶2.14](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- No rollback (¶2.27): after go-live, "if a major incident occurred, TSB would be unable to roll back on to the LBG Platform". — [FCA Final Notice ¶2.27](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- Schedule pressure and governance (¶2.10-2.12): plans built on an "insufficiently 'left-to-right' basis"; TSB publicly announced a Q1 2018 date nine days after resolving to re-plan and before finishing the re-plan. — [FCA Final Notice ¶2.10-2.12](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- Harm: about 5.2 million customers in the relevant period; 225,492 complaints; £32,705,762 redress (summary section and section 4). — [FCA Final Notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)
- Slaughter and May independent review (published by TSB 19 November 2019): **not read**. The PDF on TSB's site is image-only (no text layer) and could not be extracted. TSB's own release page **[FETCH]** says the board disputes parts of the report and attributes a key cause to two data centres "configured inconsistently". Press coverage **[SNIPPET]** reports the review criticised the largely single-event choice and testing; treat those as secondary. — [TSB release page](https://www.tsb.co.uk/news-releases/slaughter-and-may/); [report PDF, unread](https://www.tsb.co.uk/content/dam/tsb-public/documents/media-centre/Slaughter-and-May-final-report.pdf); secondary: [ITPro](https://www.itpro.com/business-strategy/digital-transformation/354155/tsbs-it-disaster-pinned-on-big-bang-approach-to)
- Cuts: **For** staged/smaller change (single event, no rollback) **and for** more pre-release testing (FCA says the dropped Active-Active test would likely have found the fault). It does not show that soak in a lower environment is sufficient: the fault was a production data-centre configuration inconsistency, which only a production-like or production test finds.

**RBS / NatWest / Ulster Bank batch failure, June 2012.** Financial Conduct Authority, Final Notice to Royal Bank of Scotland plc, National Westminster Bank plc and Ulster Bank Ltd, 19 November 2014. **[FULL-TEXT]** for cited paragraphs. — [FCA Final Notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)

- Penalty: FCA £42,000,000 for breaches of Principle 3 (¶1.1; £60 million before the 30% Stage 1 discount, ¶1.2). A separate PRA penalty exists; not read. Redress about £70.3 million to UK customers (¶2.16). At least 6.5 million UK customers affected (¶2.8). — [FCA Final Notice](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- The change (¶4.6): on Sunday 17 June 2012 Technology Services upgraded the batch scheduler for NatWest and Ulster Bank from Version 1 to Version 2A (2 plus a patch) because the old version "could no longer be sufficiently supported". — [FCA Final Notice ¶4.6](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- First full batch run on Monday 18 June showed anomalies (high mainframe CPU, batch terminal failures) (¶4.7). — [FCA Final Notice ¶4.7](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- The back-out caused the incident (¶2.9, ¶4.8-4.9): on Tuesday 19 June the team backed out the upgrade; Version 2A was not compatible with Version 1. "Technology Services had only tested the consequences of backing out Version 2 to Version 1." "This was the underlying cause of the IT Incident." — [FCA Final Notice ¶4.8](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- Second testing error (section 4, sub-paragraph (3)(b) before ¶4.25): back-out tests "did not use representative data in the queues". — [FCA Final Notice, section 4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- Blast radius (same section): one batch scheduler served both NatWest and Ulster Bank, and interdependencies reached the RBS scheduler; the FCA faults failure to reduce jobs per scheduler and to separate schedulers. — [FCA Final Notice, section 4](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- Principle 3 breach framed as inadequate "policies for testing software" and incident processes (¶2.11). — [FCA Final Notice ¶2.11](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)
- Cuts: **Against** "rollback is the safety net" unless the rollback path is itself tested with representative data; **for** blast-radius reduction (cells, separate schedulers), which is the staged-exposure logic; **for** pre-release testing of the exact artefact (2A, not 2). Stateful batch queues are the kind of state that outlives a rollback.

**Nasdaq / Facebook IPO, 18 May 2012.** SEC, "In the Matter of The NASDAQ Stock Market, LLC and NASDAQ Execution Services, LLC", Exchange Act Release No. 69655, File No. 3-15339, 29 May 2013. **[FULL-TEXT]** for cited paragraphs. — [SEC order](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)

- Design limitation (¶10): cancellations arriving during the cross calculation forced re-calculation; continued cancellations could produce "a repeated cycle of validation checks and re-calculations – known as a 'loop'". — [SEC order ¶10](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)
- Testing was done but not at scale (¶12): in the prior week Nasdaq tested "in both live trading and test environments", including intraday test crosses in production with a test symbol, but "limited the total number of orders that could be received in the test security to 40,000 orders"; on the day members entered "over 496,000 orders". — [SEC order ¶12](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)
- Stale code (¶15-16): a "randomization" delay removed from Nasdaq's rule in 2007 "had never been removed from NASDAQ's systems", so every IPO cross since 31 August 2007 ran in contravention of its rule. — [SEC order ¶15-16](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)
- The live fix (¶22-25): engineers did not know the cause; the SVP was "unaware of the existence of the validation check"; Nasdaq failed over to a duplicate matching engine with "several lines of code" removed; it "had not tested for this situation"; the executive approved it "within a few minutes" while "no one on the Code Blue call knew the precise cause". — [SEC order ¶22-25](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)
- Penalty: $10 million; undertakings include a new quality-assurance organisation focused on "integration of testing of trading systems" and standardized change-management software (¶72-73). — [SEC order, Section IV and ¶72-73](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)
- Cuts: **For** more representative pre-release testing (12x the tested volume arrived) and **for** removing stale code; **against** improvising untested changes in production. A single IPO cross cannot be percentage-ramped, so this case marks a boundary of the ramp model rather than a refutation.

**BATS IPO, 23 March 2012.** BATS Global Markets, message from CEO Joe Ratterman with Q&A, 25 March 2012 (copy hosted by Cboe). **[FULL-TEXT]**, company statement, not a regulator finding. — [BATS CEO message](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/press_releases/Message_From_BATS_CEO_Ratterman_FINAL.pdf)

- Cause as stated: "A software bug related to facilitating IPO auctions. This was the first corporate IPO on the BATS Exchange." — [BATS CEO message](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/press_releases/Message_From_BATS_CEO_Ratterman_FINAL.pdf)
- Testing claim: "While we had spent months testing our system, an unforeseen bug appeared during the BATS IPO auction that caused the system failure." — [BATS CEO message](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/press_releases/Message_From_BATS_CEO_Ratterman_FINAL.pdf)
- Spillover: three erroneous prints triggered a single-stock circuit breaker halting Apple for five minutes; BATS withdrew its IPO. — [BATS CEO message](https://cdn.cboe.com/resources/press_releases/Message_From_BATS_CEO_Ratterman_FINAL.pdf)
- Cuts: **For** the thesis in one respect (months of pre-release testing did not find the bug; first real use did) and **against** in another (the first production use was the firm's own highest-stakes event, with no lower-stakes first exposure). No regulator order on this specific failure was found.

**NYSE trading halt, 8 July 2015.** NYSE statement as reproduced by Graham Cluley, 13 July 2015. **[FETCH] of a secondary reproduction**; I did not locate the statement on nyse.com. — [Cluley reproduction](https://grahamcluley.com/nyse-bad-software-rollout/)

- As reproduced: "On Tuesday evening, the NYSE began the rollout of a software release" for the 11 July industry test of the SIP timestamp requirement; after 7am Wednesday there were communication issues because "customer gateways were not loaded with the proper configuration compatible with the new release." Trading was suspended at 11:32am and resumed at 3:10pm. — [Cluley reproduction](https://grahamcluley.com/nyse-bad-software-rollout/)
- SEC Press Release 2018-31, 6 March 2018 **[FETCH]**: NYSE and two affiliates paid $14 million for multiple episodes, including describing prices as "automated" despite extensive system issues before a total shutdown of two exchanges, and the "first-ever charged violation of Regulation SCI" (business continuity/disaster recovery). The release does not name 8 July 2015; I did not read the underlying order. — [SEC press release 2018-31](https://www.sec.gov/news/press-release/2018-31)
- Cuts: **Mixed.** A release rolled out overnight to part of the estate with a configuration mismatch against the rest is a version-skew failure during a partial rollout, i.e. a risk that staged rollouts introduce. It also shows an exchange preparing for an industry-wide weekend test (retained pre-production practice).

### Inferences

- In three of five cases (Knight, RBS, NYSE) the proximate cause is *inconsistency between components during or after a change* (one server of eight, version 2A versus 1, gateways versus new release). Progressive delivery deliberately creates mixed-version states; the finance record says mixed-version compatibility must be tested explicitly, not assumed.
- TSB and Nasdaq show that the failure of pre-release testing was not its existence but its *representativeness* (one data-centre configuration; 40,000 versus 496,000 orders). This supports the essay's claim that lower environments cannot settle production-scale questions, but the regulators' own remedy language is "test more and govern better", not "test in production".
- One-shot events (IPO cross, platform cutover without rollback, quarter-end) are the hard boundary for ramps. An honest essay should carve these out or show how to manufacture a smaller first exposure (TSB's Friends & Family pilot, Nasdaq's test-symbol crosses were attempts at exactly that and were too small).
- Management: every regulator document cited locates root cause in governance (schedule announced before re-plan complete; decisions outside the proper forum; audit unable to test back-out controls). None blames release cadence as such.

### Gaps

- Slaughter and May's TSB report could not be read (image-only PDF). PRA final notices for TSB and RBS not read; combined FCA+PRA totals are therefore not verified here.
- No primary NYSE document for July 2015 was read; the SEC's 2018 order text (as opposed to the press release) was not read.
- No SEC enforcement order specific to the BATS IPO failure was found.
- Other candidates not researched: Goldman Sachs options (August 2013), Nasdaq SIP outage (August 2013), CME, LSE, ASX CHESS replacement, Citi/Revlon.

## Configuration, flag and deployment-process outages at companies that practise progressive delivery

### Takeaway

The recurring pattern in vendors' own postmortems is not that staged rollout failed, but that a class of change (configuration, rule/content data, policy data, security updates, operator commands) travelled on a faster global path that bypassed the staged pipeline, or that staged binary rollout never exercised a dormant path. The usual remediation is "extend staged rollout, bake time and validation to config too", which supports the thesis's mechanism while contradicting any claim that flags/config are inherently low-risk; a smaller set (Roblox, Slack, Azure Front Door October 2025, Facebook's SOSP examples) shows failures that passed canary/ramp stages and appeared later or only under load.

### Cited Findings

**Class A: a fast global path with no staged rollout (config treated as not-code).**

- CrowdStrike, 19 July 2024. "External Technical Root Cause Analysis — Channel File 291", 6 August 2024 **[FULL-TEXT]**. A new IPC Template Type defined 21 input fields but sensor code supplied 20; the mismatch "evaded multiple layers of build validation and testing", partly because tests and early instances used a wildcard in the 21st field (pp. 1-2). Finding 6: "Each Template Instance should be deployed in a staged rollout." Mitigation: deployment rings with "additional bake-in time, where telemetry is gathered", plus customer control over where and when Rapid Response Content deploys (p. 6). The RCA stresses "Rapid Response Content is configuration data; it is not code or a kernel driver" (p. 8). — [CrowdStrike RCA](https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf)
- CrowdStrike, Preliminary Post Incident Review, 24 July 2024 **[FETCH]**. Sensor *code* already went through "a staged sensor rollout process that starts with dogfooding internally" with customer N/N-1/N-2 choice; content did not. Release at 04:09 UTC, reverted 05:27 UTC. Confidence rested on March 2024 testing, "trust in the checks performed in the Content Validator, and previous successful IPC Template Instance deployments". Commitments: "Implement a staggered deployment strategy for Rapid Response Content", starting with a canary; stress testing, fuzzing and fault injection. — [CrowdStrike PIR](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/falcon-content-update-preliminary-post-incident-report/)
- Cuts: **For** the thesis's mechanism (absence of ramp for one change class; remedy is rings plus bake time). **Against** any claim that data/config pushes are safe to exempt. Note the remedy also *adds pre-release testing* (fuzzing, non-wildcard tests) and gives customers a soak choice. Device-count and economic-impact figures are not in these two documents.
- Cloudflare, 2 July 2019. John Graham-Cumming (first-person; author not named in fetched text), "Details of the Cloudflare outage on July 2, 2019", 12 July 2019 **[FETCH]**. A WAF managed rule regex "backtracked enormously and exhausted CPU used for HTTP/HTTPS serving"; 27 minutes. Software normally goes DOG, PIG, Canary, global, but "by design, the WAF doesn't use this process because of the need to respond rapidly to threats"; "a change to the rules went global in seconds". "The SOP allowed a non-emergency rule change to go globally into production without a staged rollout". Test suite: "What it didn't do was test for runaway CPU utilization". 476 WAF change requests in the prior 60 days. Remedies included staged rollouts for rules (keeping an emergency global path) and performance profiling in tests. — [Cloudflare 2019](https://blog.cloudflare.com/details-of-the-cloudflare-outage-on-july-2-2019/)
- Cloudflare, 18 November 2025. Matthew Prince, "Cloudflare outage on November 18, 2025", 18 November 2025 **[FETCH]**. A ClickHouse permissions change made a query return duplicates, doubling the Bot Management feature file past a 200-feature limit; the proxy panicked. The file was regenerated every few minutes and pushed network-wide; good and bad files alternated, and the team first suspected a DDoS. Impact from about 11:20 UTC, main impact resolved 14:30, all services 17:06. "Today was Cloudflare's worst outage since 2019." Remedy: "Hardening ingestion of Cloudflare-generated configuration files in the same way we would for user-generated input" and more global kill switches. — [Cloudflare Nov 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/18-november-2025-outage/)
- Cloudflare, 5 December 2025. Dane Knecht, post dated 5 December 2025 **[FETCH]**. A WAF buffer-size change *was* being gradually deployed; engineers then disabled an internal test tool through the global configuration system, which "propagates changes within seconds to the entire fleet of servers" and "is under review following the outage we experienced on November 18." A killswitch applied to a rule type it had never been applied to hit "a straightforward error in the code, which had existed undetected for many years." About 25 minutes, about 28% of HTTP traffic. Commitments: staged rollout and versioning for config and threat-response data; fail-open; "we are locking down all changes to our network". — [Cloudflare Dec 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/)
- Cuts (Cloudflare x3): **For** applying ramps to config; **against** the assumption that an organisation with a mature staged pipeline has therefore covered its risk. Six years after committing to staged rule rollouts, a second global-config path produced two outages in three weeks. The December case is a kill switch itself causing the outage via an untested combination.
- Google Cloud, 12 June 2025 (Service Control). Google Cloud incident report **[FETCH]**. "On May 29, 2025, a new feature was added to Service Control for additional quota policy checks." The path was not exercised during region-by-region rollout because it needed a policy change to trigger; "it did not have appropriate error handling nor was it feature flag protected." On 12 June a policy with "unintended blank fields" was written and replicated globally within seconds, crash-looping every region. Root cause identified in about 10 minutes; "Within 40 minutes of the incident, the red-button rollout was completed"; us-central1 took up to about 2h40m because restarting tasks overloaded the backing store (no randomized exponential backoff). Commitments: "We will enforce all changes to critical binaries to be feature flag protected and disabled by default"; propagate globally replicated data incrementally; fail open. — [Google Cloud incident ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW](https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW)
- Cuts: **Strongly for** flags and incremental data propagation as the remedy (Google's own words). **But** it is also the Knight pattern: a staged binary rollout "baked" for two weeks without exercising the dormant path, then data activated it everywhere at once. Staged rollout and soak both give false comfort for paths that are not exercised.
- Datadog, 8 March 2023. Datadog engineering postmortem **[FETCH]**. A systemd security update was auto-applied to Ubuntu 22.04 nodes in a 06:00-07:00 UTC window; "systemd-networkd forcibly deleted the routes managed by the Container Network Interface (CNI) plugin"; tens of thousands of nodes across five regions and three clouds. Normal changes go "region-by-region, cluster-by-cluster, node-by-node", but the base image "had a legacy security update channel enabled, which caused the update to apply automatically". Remedy: channel disabled. Outage began 06:03 UTC 8 March; all services operational 9 March. — [Datadog postmortem](https://www.datadoghq.com/blog/2023-03-08-multiregion-infrastructure-connectivity-issue/)
- Cuts: **For** the mechanism (an un-staged side channel), **against** complacency: the organisation did not know the channel existed.
- AWS S3, 28 February 2017. AWS, "Summary of the Amazon S3 Service Disruption in the Northern Virginia (US-EAST-1) Region" **[FETCH]**. An operator following a playbook: "one of the inputs to the command was entered incorrectly and a larger set of servers was removed than intended." "we have not completely restarted the index subsystem or the placement subsystem in our larger regions for many years." "the tool used allowed too much capacity to be removed too quickly." Remedy: slower removal, minimum-capacity safeguards, cell partitioning. — [AWS S3 summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/)
- Facebook, 4 October 2021. Santosh Janardhan, "More details about the October 4 outage", 5 October 2021 **[FETCH]**. During routine maintenance "a command was issued with the intention to assess the availability of global backbone capacity", disconnecting all data centres; "a bug in that audit tool prevented it from properly stopping the command." Recovery was slowed by security hardening requiring physical access; the author writes "I believe a tradeoff like this is worth it." Storm drills had never simulated loss of the global backbone. — [Meta engineering 2021](https://engineering.fb.com/2021/10/05/networking-traffic/outage-details/)
- Cuts (S3, Facebook 2021): **Neutral.** Operator commands are neither flag nor release; they show that the control plane and its guardrails are the single point of failure in a fast-change culture, and that rarely exercised recovery paths rot.

**Class B: the deployment / safe-rollout / config system itself failed.**

- Facebook, 23 September 2010. Robert Johnson, "More Details on Today's Outage" **[FETCH]**. An automated system for verifying configuration values treated a changed persistent value as invalid; every client queried the database cluster, errors were treated as invalid values, creating a feedback loop. About 2.5 hours. "The key flaw that caused this outage to be so severe was an unfortunate handling of an error condition." The system was disabled; "We're exploring new designs for this configuration system". — [Facebook 2010](https://engineering.fb.com/2010/09/23/uncategorized/more-details-on-today-s-outage/)
- Microsoft Azure AD, 28 September 2020. Microsoft's RCA as quoted by Thomas Claburn, The Register, 2 October 2020 **[FETCH of secondary; Microsoft RCA not read directly]**. A service update aimed at an internal validation ring hit all rings at once because the Safe Deployment Process had "a latent defect"; normally "changes initially target a validation ring that contains no customer data". The defect also "corrupted the deployment metadata", so automated rollback failed and rollback was manual. — [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/10/02/microsoft_azure_bug/)
- Microsoft Azure Front Door, 9 and 29 October 2025. Abhishek Tiwari, Amit Srivastava, Varun Chawla, "Azure Front Door: Implementing lessons learned following October outages", Azure Networking Blog, 18 December 2025 **[FETCH]**. 9 October: "A manual cleanup of stuck tenant metadata bypassed our configuration protection layer". 29 October: incompatible metadata from two control-plane versions *passed health checks through the staged rollout*, updated the "last known good" snapshot, and then an asynchronous data-plane process crashed globally. Remedies: protection always on, synchronous validation, "more stages and longer bake times", propagation slowed to about 45 minutes from a historical 5-10 minutes. — [Azure Networking Blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azurenetworkingblog/-/4479416)
- Google Cloud, 2 June 2019. Google Cloud Networking Incident #19009 **[FETCH]**. Per the status-page report: network control-plane jobs were configured to stop during a maintenance event type, and a bug in maintenance automation allowed it "to deschedule multiple independent software clusters at once", across locations. Debugging tools shared the congested network. Remedies include extending "fail static" operation. (A commonly cited description of a config change applied beyond its intended scope comes from a separate Google blog post that I did not read.) — [Google incident 19009](https://status.cloud.google.com/incident/cloud-networking/19009)
- AWS Kinesis, 25 November 2020. AWS, "Summary of the Amazon Kinesis Event in the Northern Virginia (US-EAST-1) Region" **[FETCH]**. Trigger: "a relatively small addition of capacity"; "the new capacity had caused all of the servers in the fleet to exceed the maximum number of threads". Restart could proceed only at a few hundred servers per hour; Cognito and CloudWatch impaired; AWS could not update its Service Health Dashboard because the tool depended on Cognito. Remedy: fewer, larger servers; thread alarms; cellularization. — [AWS Kinesis summary](https://aws.amazon.com/message/11201/)
- Cuts (Class B): **Against** treating the rollout/flag/config machinery as free. The safety system is itself software with latent defects (Azure SDP), a single point of failure (GitHub flag service, below), and a source of emergent behaviour (Facebook 2010). An essay that proposes moving risk control from calendar time into a control plane must say who tests the control plane.

**Class C: passed the canary or ramp, failed later or only under load.**

- Roblox, 28-31 October 2021. Daniel Sturman et al., "Roblox Return to Service 10/28-10/31 2021", 20 January 2022 **[FETCH]**. 73 hours. "Several months ago, we enabled a new Consul streaming feature on a subset of our services", expanded incrementally; on 27 October it was enabled on a traffic-routing backend. "The system had worked well with streaming at this level for a day before the incident started". Contention appeared only under very high read and write load; a second, unrelated BoltDB freelist issue compounded it. "There was a circular dependency between our telemetry systems and Consul". — [Roblox postmortem](https://about.roblox.com/newsroom/2022/01/roblox-return-to-service-10-28-10-31-2021/)
- Slack, 22 February 2022. Laura Nolan et al., "Slack's Incident on 2-22-22", 26 April 2022 **[FETCH]**. A percentage-based Consul agent upgrade (25% steps) had completed two steps the prior week without incident; the third coincided with peak load, emptied cache nodes, and a scatter query overloaded the database into a metastable state. Pausing the rollout did not recover it: such failures "do not normally return to a stable state without a significant reduction in load". — [Slack engineering](https://slack.engineering/slacks-incident-on-2-22-22/)
- Facebook, Tang et al., "Holistic Configuration Management at Facebook", SOSP 2015, §6.4 **[FULL-TEXT of §6.4]**. Three examples: canary on 20 servers caught a schema mismatch; an engineer overrode a canary rejection ("It must be a false positive of the canary tool!") and caused crashes; a config error sending mobile requests down a rare path was missed "because the testing was done on a limited number of servers and the small scale testing was insufficient to cause any load issue", leading Facebook to add a canary phase on thousands of servers. Statistics over three months of high-impact incidents: "16% of the incidents were related to configuration management"; of those, Type I common errors 42%, Type II subtle errors 36%, Type III "valid config changes exposing code bugs" 22%. "Enhancing automated canary tests is a never ending battle". Config canary takes about ten minutes (§6.3). — [Tang et al. SOSP 2015](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2015/current/2015-Monterey/printable/008-tang.pdf)
- Cuts (Class C): **Against** the thesis where it implies ramps detect what soak detects. A ramp stage that looks healthy at low load or off-peak says little about peak; Roblox ran a day clean; Slack two stages clean. These are arguments for *longer holds that span a load cycle*, i.e. production soak.

**Class D: staging tested, irreversible in production.**

- Atlassian, April 2022. Atlassian, "Post-Incident Review on the Atlassian April 2022 outage" **[FETCH]**. A deletion script was given site IDs instead of app IDs; "775 Atlassian customers lost access"; up to 14 days. The script "followed our standard peer-review process" and "was tested in Staging per our standard change management processes"; staging could not have caught it because the wrong IDs did not exist there. Commitments: universal soft delete; "we will progressively move customers through the same process". — [Atlassian PIR](https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage)
- Cuts: **For** the thesis on both counts (staging gate passed and was useless; remedy is progressive rollout plus reversibility). Also a reminder that destructive data operations do not roll back.

**GitHub** **[SNIPPET only]**: GitHub's September 2025 availability report describes a 15 September 2025 Copilot incident in which "a partial deployment of a feature flag to a global rate limiter" rate-limited all requests until the flag was reverted. A later (June 2026, outside the period) status summary describes an internal feature-flag service failing so that flag checks failed. Not read in full. — [GitHub Availability Report September 2025](https://github.blog/news-insights/company-news/github-availability-report-september-2025/)

**Aggregate base rate.** Google SRE book, Introduction (Benjamin Treynor Sloss), 2016 **[FETCH]**: "SRE has found that roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system." Best practices listed: "Implementing progressive rollouts", "Quickly and accurately detecting problems", "Rolling back changes safely when problems arise". — [SRE book, Introduction](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)

### Inferences

- Tally of the cases above by cause: absence of staged rollout for a change class (CrowdStrike, Cloudflare 2019, Cloudflare Nov and Dec 2025, Datadog, Google 2025's policy data); control plane or safety system itself (Facebook 2010, Azure AD 2020, Azure Front Door, Google 2019, Cloudflare Dec 2025 kill switch); dormant path not exercised by rollout (Knight, Google 2025); passed ramp then failed under load or later (Roblox, Slack, Azure Front Door 29 Oct, Facebook's SOSP load example); irreversible data (Atlassian). No case in this set involved a stale *reused* flag other than Knight.
- The vendors' remediations converge: stage and bake config/data like code; validate internally generated config as untrusted input; fail open or fail static; global kill switches that are themselves exercised; break circular dependencies between the thing changing and the telemetry/tools needed to see and reverse it. The essay can adopt these as preconditions rather than treat them as objections.
- There is a real tension the essay must name: security and threat-response content (WAF rules, EDR content, bot signatures) is deliberately on a fast path because slow rollout has its own cost. Cloudflare and CrowdStrike both kept an emergency fast path after their incidents. Finance analogues: risk-limit changes, sanctions lists, kill switches, reference data.
- Speed changes the blast radius of mistakes in the control plane. Azure slowed config propagation from 5-10 minutes to about 45 minutes; Cloudflare froze network changes; Google froze Service Control changes. After serious incidents, progressive-delivery leaders *add* bake time.
- Management: several postmortems show human override or bypass of the safe path (SOSP canary override; Azure 9 October manual cleanup; Cloudflare SOP permitting non-emergency global rule change). Culture and incentives around the "fast lane" decide whether a ramp exists in practice.

### Gaps

- Microsoft's own Azure AD RCA (tracking ID SM79-F88) and the Azure Front Door PIRs were not read directly; Azure AD is via The Register's quotations.
- Google's June 2019 blog post (the "configuration change applied beyond intended scope" narrative) not read; only the status-page report.
- CrowdStrike's number of affected devices and Datadog/Cloudflare financial impact are not in the documents read.
- GitHub incidents are snippet-level only. Slack's 4 January 2021 outage and GitHub's October 2018 incident were not researched.
- I found no vendor postmortem attributing an outage to having ramped *instead of* soaking in pre-production; the evidence is about gaps in ramp coverage, not a controlled comparison.

## Deliberately retained soak, bake time, slow rollouts and extensive pre-release testing

### Takeaway

The organisations that invented progressive delivery did not abolish waiting; they moved most of it into production under limited exposure ("bake time", "baking the binary", channels, rings) and kept pre-production stages for what can be checked there. Amazon's documented default pipeline takes about four or five business days to reach all regions, with explicit hour-denominated bake periods and deployment-window calendars, which is longer than the finance team's three-day promotion; the difference is where the time is spent and what evidence is gathered during it.

### Cited Findings

- AWS. Clare Liguori, "Automating safe, hands-off deployments", Amazon Builders' Library (publication date not verified on the page; the old aws.amazon.com URL now redirects) **[FETCH]**. Pre-production: alpha and beta (functional API and end-to-end tests), gamma (production-like, multi-Region, with monitoring, alarms and canaries); some pipelines add a "zeta" stage calling only production endpoints. Production: one-box (typically at most 10% of a Region/AZ's traffic), then waves: a low-volume Region, a high-volume Region, then 3, up to 12, then the rest, always one AZ or cell at a time. — [Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- AWS bake time, same source, "Bake time" section. Reason: "Sometimes a negative impact caused by a deployment is not readily apparent." Typical values: at least one hour after each one-box stage, at least 12 hours after the first regional wave, at least two to four hours after each later wave. Bake also requires a minimum number of data points (for example at least 100 requests to an API), not time alone. Default pipelines "will deploy a change to all Regions in about four or five business days." — [Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- AWS deployment windows, same source. Deployments start only in set windows; nights, holidays and weekends excluded because "Oncall engineers typically take longer to engage at night, on office holidays, and on weekends"; many teams also avoid Fridays and late afternoons. The article notes the trade-off that very small windows make changes pile up. — [Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- AWS on flags/config, same source. Runtime configuration such as rate limits and feature flags goes through its own pipeline using the same safe-deployment techniques, on the reasoning that a bad flag can hurt production as much as bad code. — [Builders' Library](https://builder.aws.com/content/3ErTKQOTKc5NIw031UePBPxTQ6I/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments)
- Cuts: **Both.** Supports the thesis that soak belongs in production under limited exposure with automatic rollback. Cuts against "ramps are faster than trains": four to five business days, calendar windows, and pre-production stages all remain. Soak here is *in production*, measured in hours plus data points.
- Google. Alex Perry and Max Luebbe, "Testing for Reliability", SRE book ch. 17, 2016 **[FETCH]**. "A canary test isn't really a test; rather, it's structured user acceptance." "We commonly refer to the incubation period for the upgraded servers as 'baking the binary.'" Canary "isn't perfect and doesn't always catch newly introduced faults." Frequently changed configuration "can be a major risk if these changes are not treated the same as application releases." The chapter gives a model for exponential rollout and for estimating fault order from reports after rollback. — [SRE book ch. 17](https://sre.google/sre-book/testing-reliability/)
- Google. Rhandeev Singh, Sebastian Kirsch, Vivek Rau, "Reliable Product Launches at Scale", SRE book ch. 27, 2016 **[FETCH]**. "Almost all updates to Google's services proceed gradually"; a change failing validation is "automatically rolled back". Flag frameworks ramp "from 0% to 100% of users", typically first "between 1 and 10 percent". Launch checklists are retained: "Every question's importance must be substantiated, ideally by a previous launch disaster." A working configuration in a test instance does not guarantee it works live. — [SRE book ch. 27](https://sre.google/sre-book/reliable-product-launches/)
- Cuts: **For** the thesis in mechanism; **against** it in tone: Google keeps checklists and launch review alongside ramps.
- Facebook mobile. Chuck Rossi et al., "Continuous Deployment of Mobile Software at Facebook", FSE 2016 **[FULL-TEXT of §2 release-cycle passages]**. iOS release branch cut every two weeks; "The release branch is first stabilized over a period of five days"; dogfood builds to employees three times a day; then "the code is frozen and a 'soak' period of three days begins. During the soak period, only fixes for launch-blocking issues are accepted." Android: weekly cut, alpha from master and beta from the release branch to external users. Abstract-level claim: dog-fooding and feedback from alpha and beta customers "is critical to maintaining release quality." — [Rossi et al. FSE 2016](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Rossi-FSE-16.pdf)
- Cuts: **Against a simple reading of the thesis.** Where rollback is not instant (app-store binaries on user devices), the company most associated with flags kept a release train, a freeze and a soak. The soak population is real users (employees, alpha, beta), i.e. soak by population rather than by lab environment. The essay already cites this.
- Chrome. Chromium project, "Chrome Release Cycle" (docs/process/release_cycle.md, main branch as fetched 10 November 2026; contents change over time and older revisions describe six- and four-week cycles) **[FETCH, first 100,000 characters]**. Milestone branches are cut and stabilized for weeks in beta before stable; an "early stable" goes to a small share of users; stable rollout is staged and "generally reaches all users within one to two weeks"; features not code-complete at branch point wait for the next milestone. — [Chromium release cycle](https://chromium.googlesource.com/chromium/src/+/main/docs/process/release_cycle.md)
- Cuts: **Both.** A release train with soak-by-population (canary, dev, beta, stable channels), combined with heavy use of flags (Rahman et al. count over 2,400 toggles). Evidence that trains and flags coexist.
- CrowdStrike, both before and after July 2024: sensor releases already had staged rollout, internal dogfooding, and customer-selected N, N-1, N-2 versions; the remediation adds rings with "bake-in time" for content and customer control of timing. — [CrowdStrike PIR](https://www.crowdstrike.com/en-us/blog/falcon-content-update-preliminary-post-incident-report/); [CrowdStrike RCA p. 6](https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf)
- Cloudflare's normal software path (DOG employee PoP, PIG small free-customer slice, Canary, then global) "can take hours or days" per the 2019 post's description. — [Cloudflare 2019](https://blog.cloudflare.com/details-of-the-cloudflare-outage-on-july-2-2019/)
- Azure Front Door after October 2025: "more stages and longer bake times"; config propagation deliberately slowed. — [Azure Networking Blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azurenetworkingblog/-/4479416)
- Facebook config: about ten minutes of automated canary per config change is accepted as "needed in order to reliably determine whether the application is healthy" (SOSP 2015 §6.3). — [Tang et al. SOSP 2015](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2015/current/2015-Monterey/printable/008-tang.pdf)
- Securities industry test weekends **[SNIPPET, SIFMA pages via search; rule text not read]**: SIFMA has led an annual industry-wide business continuity test since 2003, held on a Saturday in October (2025 test: Saturday 25 October 2025); Regulation SCI requires SCI entities to designate members to participate in annual BC/DR testing, run alongside it. The NYSE July 2015 release was itself preparation for an 11 July industry test. — [SIFMA industry-wide test page](https://www.sifma.org/resources/general/industry-wide-business-continuity-test); [Cluley reproduction of NYSE statement](https://grahamcluley.com/nyse-bad-software-rollout/)
- Cuts: **Against** the thesis for multi-party interface changes. When many independent firms must change together, production cannot be ramped unilaterally; coordinated weekend tests in production-like or actual production venues are the industry's substitute.
- LinkedIn experimentation guidance prescribes a minimum hold: "keep their experiment at MPR for at least a week, and longer if burn-in effect is present" (Xu, Duan, Huang, KDD 2018, §3 Principle 2). — [SQR, arXiv 1801.08532](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.08532)

### Inferences

- Reasons given for retained waiting, in the sources' own terms: slow-burning effects not visible at deploy time (AWS); need for enough data points on low-traffic paths (AWS); on-call availability (AWS windows); irreversibility of shipped binaries (Facebook mobile, Chrome, CrowdStrike sensor); load-dependent behaviour (Facebook config, Roblox, Slack); weekly cycles and novelty effects in user behaviour (LinkedIn); cross-firm coordination (SIFMA, Reg SCI).
- Where the soak happens: AWS, Google, Cloudflare, Azure, CrowdStrike content rings and LinkedIn soak *in production under limited exposure*. Facebook mobile and Chrome soak with *real users on pre-release channels*. AWS gamma, CrowdStrike sensor QA, exchange test weekends and finance UAT remain *pre-production*. No source describes eliminating pre-production stages.
- The finance team's "three days of promotion and soak, twice-weekly trains" is not obviously slower than Amazon's four-to-five-day default. The stronger form of the essay's claim is about *what the elapsed time measures* (real traffic with automatic rollback and data-point thresholds versus idle time in a lower environment) and about decoupling deploy from release, not about total elapsed time.
- Management: AWS encodes the conservative judgment (bake hours, windows, data-point minimums) in the pipeline so that it is not renegotiated per release. That is a governance design, and arguably closer to a release train with automated gates than to ad hoc flag flipping.

### Gaps

- Not researched for lack of time: Windows Insider rings and Microsoft's Safe Deployment Practices documentation; Firefox release channels; firmware and embedded or safety-critical domains (automotive, avionics, medical); database/schema-migration practice (expand-contract, long dual-write periods); NYSE Pillar's symbol-by-symbol migration. These are plausible further counter-evidence but nothing is asserted about them here.
- The Builders' Library article's publication date and the exact bake-time figures should be re-verified on the page before quoting; they came through fetch-tool extraction.
- I did not find a Google SRE passage stating that risky infrastructure rollouts take weeks or months; the fetched chapter 27 text does not say this.
- Regulation SCI rule text (17 CFR 242.1004) was not read.

## Known limits of canaries, ramps and flags as documented by practitioners and researchers

### Takeaway

Practitioner and academic sources agree that canaries supplement rather than replace testing, that their detection power depends on traffic volume, duration and load at the time, and that flags carry a long-lived cost in combinatorial test burden and removal debt. The specific blind spots (rare paths, load-dependent and time-dependent behaviour, batch work units, non-reversible state, dependence on the flag/config service) map closely onto finance concerns such as month-end batch and low-volume instruments.

### Cited Findings

- Google SRE Workbook, "Canarying Releases", Alec Warner and Štěpán Davidovič with Alex Hidalgo, Betsy Beyer, Kyle Smith, Matt Duftler (O'Reilly, 2018; year not verified on the page) **[FETCH]**. Definition: "We define canarying as a partial and time-limited deployment of a change in a service and its evaluation." Positioning: "Canarying can be a simple, robust, and easily integratable way to supplement testing." "Some defects will reach production." — [SRE Workbook, Canarying Releases](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
- Same chapter, "Choosing a Canary Population and Duration": the canary "should be sizeable and last long enough to be representative of the overall deployment"; "We need to receive enough traffic on the system to ensure it has handled a representative sample"; "Performance defects typically manifest only under heavy load". Cadence constraint: "If you release daily, you can't let your canary last for a week while running only one canary deployment at a time." and "We strongly advise running only one canary deployment at a time." — [SRE Workbook, Canarying Releases](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
- Same chapter, "Before/After Evaluation Is Risky": "Because time is one of the biggest sources of change in observed metrics"; a Monday release may compare "behavior during a business day to behavior during a weekend". "Canarying in Noninteractive Systems": "make sure the canary duration at minimum spans the duration of a single work unit." "Metrics Should Indicate Problems": overly strict criteria give "lots of false positives". Error-budget cost is "directly proportional to the amount of traffic exposed to defects". The chapter also notes blue/green, synthetic load and traffic teeing are not true canaries and have limits (e.g. poor state coverage). — [SRE Workbook, Canarying Releases](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/)
- Cuts: **Against** the thesis for batch and periodic workloads. The "single work unit" rule means a canary for a daily batch needs at least a day, for month-end at least a month-end. It also states an explicit conflict between canary duration and release frequency.
- Google SRE book ch. 17: canary "isn't perfect and doesn't always catch newly introduced faults." — [SRE book ch. 17](https://sre.google/sre-book/testing-reliability/)
- Facebook SOSP 2015 §6.4: small-scale canary missed a load problem on a rare code path; 22% of config-related high-impact incidents were valid config changes exposing latent code bugs; an engineer overrode a correct canary rejection. — [Tang et al. SOSP 2015](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2015/current/2015-Monterey/printable/008-tang.pdf)
- Ramps that passed and then failed under load or later: Roblox (a day clean), Slack (two 25% steps clean), Azure Front Door 29 October 2025 (health checks passed, asynchronous crash afterwards). — [Roblox](https://about.roblox.com/newsroom/2022/01/roblox-return-to-service-10-28-10-31-2021/); [Slack](https://slack.engineering/slacks-incident-on-2-22-22/); [Azure Networking Blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azurenetworkingblog/-/4479416)
- Dormant paths are invisible to both rollout and soak: Google June 2025 (path needed a policy change to trigger), Knight (path needed RLP orders). — [Google incident](https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW); [SEC order ¶15-16](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- State that outlives rollback: RBS back-out left jobs missing from batch queues (¶4.9); Atlassian deletions took up to 14 days to restore; Azure AD 2020 rollback metadata corrupted; Knight's rollback worsened the event. — [FCA RBS notice ¶4.9](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf); [Atlassian PIR](https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage); [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/2020/10/02/microsoft_azure_bug/); [SEC order ¶27](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf)
- Ramp-duration guidance from experimentation. Ya Xu, Weitao Duan, Shaochen Huang, "SQR: Balancing Speed, Quality and Risk in Online Experiments", KDD 2018 (arXiv 1801.08532) **[FULL-TEXT of §3 principles]**. Four phases: pre-MPR ramps for risk mitigation; the Maximum Power Ramp (usually 50%) for measurement; optional post-MPR ramps for operational risk; optional long-term holdout for learning. Both errors are named: "we ramp too slowly and much time and resource is wasted; or we ramp too fast". Pre-MPR: "we should not be 'waiting' for statistical significance at any pre-MPR ramps." At MPR, hold at least a week to cover weekly cycles and burn-in. A one-day experiment is "biased towards heavy users". — [SQR, arXiv 1801.08532](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.08532)
- Cuts: **Both.** Supports ramping quickly through early stages once risk is judged small (against over-long soak), while prescribing a minimum one-week hold for trustworthy measurement and acknowledging time-dependent effects.
- Flag combinatorics and testing. Jens Meinicke, Chu-Pan Wong, Bogdan Vasilescu, Christian Kästner, "Exploring Differences and Commonalities between Feature Flags and Configuration Options", ICSE-SEIP 2020 (nine semi-structured interviews) **[FULL-TEXT of §4 passages]**. The number of configurations "grows exponentially with the number of configuration decisions"; interviewee I6: flags "lead to a lot of accidental complexity"; I7: more than 20 flags per team is "worrisome". On testing: "many teams make changes to configurations without testing the specific configurations – which is dangerous"; interviewees "sometimes rapidly change to untested configurations in emergency situations, e.g., to quickly roll back a feature". — [Meinicke et al. 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10157116)
- Flag removal debt, same paper: removal "was consistently identified as the key challenge for feature flags"; I1: "One of the big drivers of tech debt that we have is still code paths sticking around under a feature flag that's not being used anymore." — [Meinicke et al. 2020](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10157116)
- Cuts: **Against.** The kill-switch state is frequently an *untested configuration*, precisely when it is needed (compare Cloudflare December 2025, Knight ¶27). Small sample (nine interviews), qualitative.
- Flag lifetime. Md Tajmilur Rahman, Louis-Philippe Querel, Peter C. Rigby, Bram Adams, "Feature Toggles: Practitioner Practices and a Case Study", MSR 2016 **[FULL-TEXT of cited passages]**. Chrome, 39 releases over five years: "over 2.4 thousand distinct toggles"; "On average 72% of the toggles survive 5 or more releases"; "half of the toggles surviving 12 or more releases"; a long tail survived all five years. On testing: "The larger the number of feature toggles, the more possible combinations of features must be tested"; one third of Chrome toggles relate to testing and development. The paper cites Knight as an example of disabled-but-present code (its description of Knight's business is inaccurate, see above). — [Rahman et al. MSR 2016](https://users.encs.concordia.ca/~pcr/paper/Rahman2016MSR.pdf)
- Cuts: **Against** the "flags are temporary" assumption; one large open-source project, release toggles mixed with long-lived switches, so not directly a web-service result.
- Dependency on the flag/config/control plane: Cloudflare's global configuration system (seconds to whole fleet); Roblox telemetry dependent on Consul; AWS unable to post to its status dashboard in both 2017 and 2020; GitHub flag-service failure (snippet only). — [Cloudflare Dec 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/); [Roblox](https://about.roblox.com/newsroom/2022/01/roblox-return-to-service-10-28-10-31-2021/); [AWS S3](https://aws.amazon.com/message/41926/); [AWS Kinesis](https://aws.amazon.com/message/11201/)
- Aggregate: Google's "roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system" is a statement about change in general, made by the organisation that then prescribes progressive rollout as the response; it is not evidence that slower change is safer. — [SRE book, Introduction](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)

### Inferences

- Mapping to finance: (1) low-volume instruments, rare order types and corporate-action paths have too little traffic for a short canary to have statistical power (AWS's "at least 100 requests" rule and the Workbook's representative-sample rule make this explicit); (2) end-of-day, month-end and quarter-end batch are "work units" that a canary must span; (3) settlement, ledger and position state can outlive a rollback; (4) market open is a load step that off-hours stages do not exercise (Slack, Knight's pre-open orders bypassing the price collar).
- The honest reformulation for the essay: ramps replace *idle* soak well for high-traffic, stateless, reversible changes; they do not replace time-in-state for periodic, low-traffic or irreversible behaviour. For those, the practitioners above keep a long hold at limited exposure (production soak), shadow/dual-run with reconciliation (Netflix's consistency-checking example quoted in Rahman et al.), or pre-production tests at representative scale.
- Flag hygiene is a management problem more than a tooling one: both academic papers find developers intend to remove flags and do not, and that emergency flips use untested combinations. A policy that every kill-switch state is tested regularly, and that flags have owners and expiry, addresses the Knight and Cloudflare-December failure modes directly.
- Canary overrides (SOSP example) and fast-lane exceptions (Cloudflare SOP) show the limiting factor is often organisational permission to bypass, not detection capability.
