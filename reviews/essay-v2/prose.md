# Prose review: essay v2

Reviewed: `essay.md` at commit 941fe30, about 5,400 words of body text. Line numbers refer to that file.

## Summary judgement

The commissioner is right about the impression and wrong about the cause. The essay has none of the usual vocabulary of machine prose: no "delve", no "landscape", no dash confetti, no inflated adjectives. Its facts are specific and its numbers are real. Section 5 contains a real argument, and the re-reading of Knight Capital in section 9 is an actual idea.

It reads as machine-written for four structural reasons:

1. Nobody is speaking. There is no first person outside quotations, no experience, no irritation, no joke. The narrator is "this essay", which refers to itself ten times.
2. The unit of composition is the citation. Most paragraphs are a one-line verdict followed by two to five "Firm did thing. (Source)" sentences, with nothing between them that reasons from one to the next. There are 74 parenthetical citations carrying 89 links; 30 paragraphs end on a closing bracket.
3. Sections 7, 8 and 9 are bulleted lists disguised as prose: 18 consecutive paragraphs that open with a bolded imperative or label.
4. The essay keeps covering itself. Scope notes, provenance notes and inline definitions of terms the reader uses every day interrupt the argument, in the voice of a compliance disclaimer.

The result is an annotated bibliography arranged as advice. A director finishes it knowing that forty organisations did something and not knowing what the author thinks it would be like to do it at their firm.

### 1.17 Where the usual charges do not stick

- Lists of three. There are about a dozen ("tested together, approved together and exposed together", 94; "justified, approved and time-limited", 172), but most are real inventories. Not a major tell here.
- Uniform sentence length. False: mean 19 words, with 49 sentences of eight or fewer and 49 of thirty or more. What is uniform is the sequence: short verdict, long fact, citation, repeat.
- Uniform paragraph length. Sections 1 to 5 vary. Sections 6 to 9 do not: nearly every paragraph is 55 to 155 words of the same shape.

## 2. Section verdicts

### Section 1

It states the thesis, reassures the reader that others went first, and leads with the FCA. The FCA paragraph (19) is the strongest evidence in the essay and is delivered as an abstract. No mind is visible until then. Worst paragraph, line 11:

> That is the whole claim of this essay: change, because the alternative is better overall. The alternative is to deploy small changes to production continuously, with each new behaviour switched off behind a feature flag: a setting that chooses between the old behaviour and the new without another deployment.

Diagnosis: announces a claim instead of making one, states it in the vaguest words available, then defines "feature flag" for engineering directors.

### Section 2

The best-constructed section after 5: one idea, three cases, each chosen for a reason. The Netflix quote and the Amazon fourteen days do real work, and Atlassian is a proper counter-example. Worst paragraph, line 39:

> A pre-production soak asks whether a large batch survives several days in an environment that resembles production. A ramp asks whether one small change behaves correctly on production itself, for a few clients, with a way back. The second question is the one you need answered.

Diagnosis: a symmetrical pair and an aphorism standing where the reason should be. The reason comes in the next paragraph; this one is a caption for the figure above it.

### Section 3

It defines dark launching and its two forms. Facebook Chat is told with dates and no tension; GitHub's four days of fixing mismatches is the real story and gets one sentence. Worst paragraph, line 56:

> By 2015 Facebook described the dormant rung as ordinary practice (…) Flickr had explained the same habit in 2009 (…) Netflix used it to stop acceptance testing from blocking deployment…

Diagnosis: three sources to establish that code can be deployed switched off, which nobody doubts. It reads as notes that had to go somewhere.

### Section 4

It argues that canaries, flags and experiments are one mechanism. The two cautions (client as the unit; say whether it is a safety ramp or an experiment) are useful to this reader and are buried under the history. No mind at work between lines 66 and 78. Worst paragraph, line 76 (quoted in 1.2).

Diagnosis: the intellectual history of experimentation platforms, 2007 to 2018, for a reader deciding whether to drop a soak. Four citations, no consequence drawn. Cut to one sentence.

### Section 5

The one section where the essay thinks. "While release 1 sits in pre-production for three days, everything your engineers finish goes into release 2" (94) is the argument a director can repeat in a meeting, and it comes from reasoning, not from a source. Worst paragraph, line 106:

> The batch is where the risk gathers. A study of continuous deployment at Facebook and at OANDA (…) At the banks each release bundled thousands of changes, which raised anxiety and led to much more testing; failures were harder to isolate; and bugs judged non-critical went unfixed for many months. (…) The Financial Times, the newspaper, gives a before-and-after…

Diagnosis: the most relevant comparison in the whole essay (the same software, deployed continuously by its maker and in bundles by banks) is given three sentences and a pair of semicolons, then pushed aside by a second story about a newspaper.

### Section 6

Sound content, and line 110 is a good opening. The sub-headings are the only true headings below section level and they help. From 126 onwards it is firm, quote, citation. Worst paragraph, line 120:

> The gain is large. Wealthfront, an SEC-regulated investment firm, reported in 2010 that a rollback took about five seconds across its cluster. (…) The rule also gives you something for audit: the hash that ran in test is the hash that runs in production.

Diagnosis: a verdict, one sixteen-year-old data point from a firm the reader has not heard of, and an audit point that belonged in the previous paragraph.

### Section 7

It is meant to be the heart of the essay, the part addressed to the reader's own job, and it is the most mechanical: six bolded commands, twenty citations in about 760 words. The reader is told what to do six times and never shown a manager doing it. Worst paragraph, line 148 (quoted in 1.2).

Diagnosis: "Fund the platform, and expect years" is a budget request. It needs an argument about what the money buys and what happens without it; it gets four unrelated numbers.

### Section 8

The most valuable section for this audience and the most honest: line 172, on what the rule texts do require, shows a writer who read them. Uber's recorded-response shadow is explained clearly. Worst paragraph, line 164:

> …These are slower signals than an error rate, and they set the length of a ramp stage. No source states the rule, but it follows: a change that affects settlement should hold at small exposure for at least one reconciliation cycle. Idempotent operations, which have the same effect however many times they are retried, bound the damage of a retry…

Diagnosis: the essay's one original, usable rule sits mid-paragraph between a Stripe statistic and a definition of idempotence, introduced with an apology.

### Section 9

Knight (184, 188) is the best writing in the essay: told at the right length, then re-read against the usual telling. The six entries after it are headlines, 30 to 80 words each, too short to persuade. Worst paragraph, line 196:

> Roblox had used a new streaming feature on some services for months. A day after it enabled the feature on its traffic-routing service, a 73-hour outage began. (…) Hold at least one stage through a full daily peak, and for batch work through a full run.

Diagnosis: a non sequitur. Nothing in the two sentences shows that the ramp "ended too soon" or that a daily peak was the trigger, so the instruction floats free of the case.

### Section 10

The six steps are sensible and the Amazon instruction to encode today's process is the right opening move. The gaps list is candid and unusual. Placing it immediately before the conclusion makes the essay deflate and then assert. Worst item, step 3:

> **Give one team the whole job**: build, deploy, and the pager.

Diagnosis: the hardest organisational act in the essay, the one Netflix says fails by half-measures and risks burnout, in eleven words.

### Sources and method

It does its job, and belongs in the repository, not at the end of a persuasive essay. Worst paragraph, line 233:

> Quotations were read in the source document itself; those first gathered through a summarising tool were checked again against the page, and the record of that check is in…

Diagnosis: it tells the reader the piece came out of a pipeline. The final sentence the reader sees is "has not been independently audited."

## 3. Voice

Nobody is speaking. There is no "I" or "we" outside quotations, no claim to have run a release, sat on a change board or been paged. No stakes are declared: the essay never says what happens to the reader's firm if it stays put. Humour is absent except where a source supplies it and the essay fails to notice (a team needing 45 minutes "to figure out how to turn it off"). Doubt appears only as disclaimer.

The moments that come closest to a person:

- "Five days in production observed nothing because nothing used the path, and three days of soak would have observed the same nothing." (188) An opinion with an edge, and the only sentence with a trace of sarcasm.
- "Knight is usually told as a story about reckless deployment." (188) The writer disagrees with someone.
- "And no rule text mentions feature flags, so nobody has told you how a flag change is classified." (172) Someone who has thought about the reader's next meeting with compliance.
- "A rollback nobody has practised is a new, untested change made during an incident." (128)
- "The UK regulator is more open to real experiments than most engineers assume" (80)

Stretches with no voice at all:

- Line 13, the roll-call.
- Lines 47–58: Facebook, Flickr, Netflix, GitHub, Square as a relay.
- Lines 76–88: the history of experimentation.
- All of section 7 (136–150), where a voice matters most.
- Lines 190–200: six case notes.
- Lines 217–243: gaps and method.

That is roughly half the essay.

## 4. Density and pacing

Where it drones:

- Section 4 after the table. The reader came for an argument about soaks and gets a literature review on A/B testing.
- Section 7. Every paragraph is the same height and the same shape, and the citations arrive every second sentence.
- Section 9 after Knight. Seven cases in one section means none except Knight can be told. Google Cloud, CrowdStrike, Cloudflare, RBS, Roblox, Uber's flags and TSB average under 60 words each.
- The run from line 217 to the end: a list of things nobody knows, a caveat, a summary, a method note.

Claims that needed a story:

- "Do not answer a failure with a gate" (146). The OANDA story is the best management story in the essay and is told in four sentences, then interrupted by Allspaw.
- "Move the pager" (142). Netflix's half-measure, where developers deferred to the release specialist and the specialist never had time to automate, is the reader's probable future and gets two sentences.
- OANDA's bank licensees (106). See section 5 verdict.
- Starling (144). A regulated bank whose auditors "loved" a release signal is exactly the scene this reader wants.
- RBS (194) and TSB (200): the two cases from the reader's own world, with fines and customer counts, are among the shortest entries.
- The reader's own Thursday. Line 94 is the only place the cost of the present system is shown in the reader's terms.

Stories and facts to cut or shrink:

- Facebook Chat's dates (47). Keep the dark-launch idea in a sentence.
- Flickr 2009, Netflix 2013, Dropbox 2017, Wealthfront 2010, Booking's training cost.
- Google 2010, Kohavi 2007, LinkedIn 2014, Uber 2018 (76).
- The Basel II parallel run (162): a good rhetorical point ("banks already know this"), which needs one clause, not a citation to a 2006 draft rule.
- The inline source lines under figures 6 and 12.

The citation apparatus is smothering, not helping. One citation per 70 words means the sentence has become a container for a link, and claims are cut to the size of what one source can support. For this audience sourcing is an asset and should stay, but as numbered notes, out of the line of reading. Fewer sources, each told at more length, would persuade better than the present forty. A director is convinced by one case they can retell, not by a count.

## 5. Three rewrites

The three are weak in different ways: a roll-call, a squandered story, and a citation stack. Each rewrite uses only facts already in the essay.

### 5.1 Line 13, the roll-call

Original:

> You would not be a pioneer. Facebook launched its chat system this way in 2008. Amazon, Google, Netflix and Microsoft have published their versions in detail. Uber's payments group, Square and Stripe have published staged migrations of systems that move money. Monzo and Starling have published how regulated banks release many times a day. In 2024 the handbook that US bank examiners work from described canary releases (…) and observed that "in a DevOps environment, the same number and type of changes can be made daily or even hourly".

Rewrite:

> None of this is new. Facebook launched chat this way in 2008, and for years afterwards the fair reply from a payments firm was that Facebook could afford to be wrong. That reply has worn out. Uber's payments group, Square and Stripe have each published how they moved a system that handles money across in stages. Monzo and Starling hold banking licences and release many times a day. By 2024 the handbook US bank examiners work from was describing canary releases as a matter of course. You are late to this, and the benefit of being late is that the people who went first have written down what broke.

What changed: the list now answers the objection the reader actually holds (those firms are not like us) and moves from least to most like the reader. Four names and a limp quotation are gone. The fast-follower point is made in the same breath, so the next paragraph is not needed.

### 5.2 Line 146, the squandered story

Original:

> **Do not answer a failure with a gate.** This is the test of a management team. At OANDA, after a failure that "cost the company a significant amount of money within seconds", senior management reached for more approvals and "a full-blown, separate and traditional testing team". The post-mortem found that unit tests had caught the bug and been ignored among false alarms. Engineering persuaded management to wire the test results into the deploy decision. (…) Then ask what your incident reviews reward. John Allspaw's argument…

Rewrite:

> What decides whether any of this lasts is the first expensive failure after you adopt it. OANDA had one: a bug that "cost the company a significant amount of money within seconds". Senior management asked for what senior management asks for, which was more approvals and "a full-blown, separate and traditional testing team". Then the post-mortem came back. The unit tests had caught the bug. They had failed as designed, and the failure had been ignored among the false alarms. A testing team and another signature would have added people to a process whose actual defect was that a correct answer from a machine could not stop a deploy. Engineering argued for wiring the test results into the deploy decision, and won. You will be in that meeting one day, on the management side of the table, and a gate will be the easy thing to ask for. Find out first whether something already knew.

What changed: the events are in the order they happened, so the post-mortem lands as a reversal. The writer says why the proposed gate would not have worked, which the original leaves to the reader. The bold command, "This is the test of a management team" and the Allspaw sentence are cut; Allspaw is a second subject and needs its own paragraph or none.

### 5.3 Line 144, the citation stack

Original:

> **Move approval; do not delete it.** At Amazon, "the code review is the last manual review and approval that a code change receives from an engineer". (…) The Financial Times dropped its change approval board and records each release automatically (…) Starling Bank shows how far you can go while keeping human gates. In 2018 it was releasing up to five times a day (…) In 2025 it still required three approvals for each of several daily releases, one of them a business approval.

Rewrite:

> You will not abolish approval, and Starling suggests you need not try. In 2018 the bank was releasing up to five times a day. Every engineer vouched for their own commits in chat before a release went out, and a visible signal showed that a release was in progress; of that signal its slides say "our auditors loved this one". In 2025 Starling still required three approvals for each of several daily releases, one of them from the business. Three signatures, several times a day, is only possible when the thing being signed is small enough to read. I think that is the difference between Starling's approvers and the change boards in the FCA's review, which passed more than 90% of the major changes put to them. Amazon and the Financial Times have gone further: at Amazon the code review is the last human approval a change receives, and the FT dropped its change board and records each release automatically. You do not have to follow them that far to get most of the benefit.

What changed: the bank comes first because the reader is a bank's supplier, and the paragraph is built round it. One inference is drawn and owned ("I think"), tying this section back to the FCA finding in section 1. Amazon and the FT become the far end of a range instead of two equal-weight exhibits, and the long FT quotation goes.

## 6. Opening and ending

### Opening

- Title, "Stop soaking, start ramping": a paired-imperative slogan. It also uses "soaking" and "ramping" before the reader knows the essay's sense of either, though that matters less for this audience.
- Subtitle: a second-level heading doing a subtitle's job, containing a list of three techniques. It is a table of contents.
- Dateline: "Written for engineering managers at…" tells the reader who they are, and "Research completed November 10, 2026" tells them a research process produced this. It also says "managers" where the commission says directors.
- Section heading, "You are behind, and that is an advantage": a paradox hook.
- Paragraph 1 describes the reader's own release process back to them, explains why a release train is called a train, and defines "soak". It ends "this essay argues that you should replace it".
- Paragraph 2 announces "the whole claim of this essay" and states it as "the alternative is better overall".
- Paragraph 3 is the roll-call.

Three paragraphs in, the reader has learned nothing they did not know and has met no one. The essay then says "Start with evidence from your own industry" (19), which is an admission that it has not started.

What it needs: open on the fact that should unsettle a careful director. The material is already on the page. At 23 regulated firms, in the regulator's own review, the changes that received the most oversight failed more than twice as often as changes overall, and change boards approved more than 90% of what they saw, some never rejecting anything in a year. Alternatively: Knight's dormant code sat in production for five days and nothing observed it. Either puts the reader's assumption (that waiting and approval are the cautious choice) in doubt in the first hundred words. Then state the claim once, in a voice, with what is at stake for the reader. Move the 1.6% and 3.8% out of the alt text and into the sentence.

### Ending

- Line 225, "Speed alone is no virtue either": a hedge against a claim the essay did not make, with a dangling "either" left over from the list above.
- Line 227 is a symmetrical recap ("Your three days protect clients by… The firms in this essay protect clients by…"), a repeat of "better overall", a three-item inventory of evidence, and a final sentence that points the reader back to section 7.
- The essay then continues for 170 words of method, and its true last words are "has not been independently audited".
- The gaps list sits directly before the conclusion, so the reader is told five things nobody knows and then told the case is made.

What it needs: no summary. A reader who has come this far knows the argument. End on the act or on the image. The act is step 1: measure the three days and see how much of it is anyone testing anything; Amazon's answer was two days in sixteen. The image is Knight's eighth server, or the "same nothing". The gaps deserve to be owned in the author's voice and turned to the reader's advantage: no incumbent with a mainframe core has published how it did this, so the firm that does it carefully will be the one others cite. Put that before the steps, not after. Move the method note to the README or a linked page.

## 7. Rules for the next draft

1. Write as someone. Use "I" where there is a judgement, and say what you would do in the reader's position and what worries you about it.
2. No bolded sentence openers. If a section needs sub-headings, use real ones, as section 6 does; otherwise write paragraphs that follow from each other.
3. Halve the cases and double their length. Every organisation named must do something on the page. If it cannot be given three sentences of story, it goes in a note.
4. Move citations to numbered notes. No paragraph ends on a bracket.
5. Between any two pieces of evidence, write the sentence that says what the second adds to the first. If there is no such sentence, cut one of them.
6. Do not define soak, canary, feature flag, change board or idempotence for people who run a release train at a payments firm.
7. Never write "this essay". Never refer to a section by number.
8. One caveat per claim, placed where a sceptic would raise it and phrased as a concession by a person, not as a note on research method. Keep the constraints in section 8; they are a strength.
9. Do not count your points in advance ("two cautions", "three parts"). Allow one deliberate "not X but Y" and one symmetrical pair per section, at most.
10. Quote only where the wording is the evidence or the speaker is audible. Paraphrase the rest.
11. Put the reader's firm on the page: the Thursday release, the person who chairs the change board, the client with a notice period. Show the cost of the present system in their terms before prescribing anything.
12. Cut the history of experimentation, the recap paragraph and the method note from the essay proper. Open on the FCA finding or on Knight. End on something to do on Monday.
