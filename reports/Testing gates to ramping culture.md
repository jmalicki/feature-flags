# Leaders moved the soak into production

The organizations that replaced testing periods did not stop waiting; they moved the wait out of a lower environment and into production, behind limited exposure, explicit metrics and a rehearsed way back. Amazon's documented default pipeline still takes **four to five business days** to reach every Region, which is longer than a three-day promotion, and it still has alpha, beta and gamma stages; what changed is that the elapsed time is spent on real traffic at bounded blast radius, one small change at a time, with automatic rollback ([Liguori, Amazon Builders' Library, 2020](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)). The case that this is better overall rests on four firm legs: staging cannot reproduce production scale, data and configuration; batching several days of change is itself a measured risk; the UK conduct regulator found, in over one million 2019 changes at 23 financial firms, that smaller and more frequent releases succeeded more often while "major" changes failed at **3.8% against 1.6%** despite extra scrutiny and change boards approved over 90% of what they saw ([FCA, 2021](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/multi-firm-reviews/implementing-technology-change)); and no regulatory text read for this report (PCI DSS v4.0, FFIEC 2024, EBA 2019, EU DORA and its RTS, MAS TRM 2021, PCAOB AS 2201, PRA SS1/21) requires a soak period, a release train or multi-day environment promotion. Managers made the transition by moving ownership and the pager to the authoring team, relocating approval to peer review plus machine-generated evidence, funding a platform, and making incident review non-punitive; every regulated firm that has published its design kept named human approvers. The evidence is weak in places the essay must respect: no source measures incidents before and after a payments firm left release trains, no one has published defect yield per day of soak or detection rates for canaries, period-boundary batch (end-of-day, month-end, settlement cut-offs) is essentially undocumented, and no firm has published an auditor's or examiner's verdict on its automated controls. The failure record (Knight, RBS, TSB, CrowdStrike, Cloudflare, Google Cloud, Roblox, Slack) does not rehabilitate the testing period; it shows that ramps fail where a change class bypasses them, where a dormant path is never exercised, and where the undo path was never tested.

## Fifteen years of first-person accounts show the wait being relocated, not removed

### The before states were measured, and most of the elapsed time was people waiting

The "before" in these accounts looks like the audience's present. Amazon measured, more than a decade before its 2019 write-up, that code took **16 days on average from check-in to production, of which almost 14 were waiting** for someone to start a build, perform a deployment or run tests ([Mansour, Builders' Library, 2019](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/going-faster-with-continuous-delivery/)). Netflix's Edge group in 2012 had operations specialists who deployed and ran code, releases "on the order of weeks", and canaries that had decayed into a week of waiting judged by "nothing broke after one week of canarying, let's push it"; the authors call that "verifying endurance" instead of correct functionality ([Netflix, 2018](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249)). LinkedIn's mobile app shipped monthly after "a manual, four-day regression test suite", with engineers rushing code in before the deadline or waiting another month ([LinkedIn, 2016](https://engineering.linkedin.com/blog/2016/02/3x3--speeding-up-mobile-releases)). Etsy's web push took "3 developers, 1 operations engineer, everyone else on standby and over an hour" before 2010 ([Kastner, 2010](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/quantum-of-deployment)). The Azure DevOps team's full test run took "the better part of a day", with days or weeks to repair tests broken by legitimate changes ([Harry, 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)). The Financial Times released about 12 times a year, on Saturdays, through a change approval board ([Wells, QCon London 2019](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/microservices-financial-times/)).

### The mechanisms arrived in three bands, and each company kept more structure than the legend says

The dated record clusters in three bands. From 2008 to 2010 the vocabulary appeared: Facebook's dark launch of Chat ([2008](https://engineering.fb.com/2008/05/13/web/facebook-chat/)), IMVU's fifty deploys a day behind a nine-minute test suite ([Fitz, 2009](https://timothyfitz.com/2009/02/10/continuous-deployment-at-imvu-doing-the-impossible-fifty-times-a-day/)), Flickr's flags and "10+ deploys per day" ([Allspaw and Hammond, 2009](https://paulhammond.org/2009/06/velocity/10deploys.pdf)), Wealthfront's one-instance-first ramp with five-second automatic rollback at an SEC- and FINRA-regulated firm ([Fortunato, 2010](https://eng.wealthfront.com/?p=1273)), and Humble and Farley's rule that the binary deployed to production "should be exactly the same" as the one that passed acceptance testing ([Continuous Delivery, ch. 5](https://www.informit.com/articles/article.aspx?p=1621865&seqNum=3)). From 2012 to 2016 came scale reports and the first empirical papers: Netflix's automated canary score after an eight-hour canary ([2013](https://netflixtechblog.com/deploying-the-netflix-api-79b6176cc3f0)), Azure DevOps's "ring 0" built after public outages ([Harry, 2014](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/visual-studio-online-update-feb-10/)), Facebook's configuration canaries ([Tang et al., SOSP 2015](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2015/current/2015-Monterey/008-tang-online.pdf)), and the Facebook and OANDA study ([Savor et al., ICSE 2016](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)). From 2017 to 2022 the practice was codified as pipelines with explicit bake times and regulators began to report on it: Facebook's push-from-master ([Rossi, 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)), Amazon's wave pipeline (2020), the FCA review (2021) and Monzo's automated rollback across 2,100 services ([Monzo, 2022](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/11/02/argo-rollouts-at-scale)).

None of the exemplars deleted pre-release controls. Amazon kept alpha, beta and a production-like gamma stage, a bake of at least one hour after each one-box stage and at least 12 hours after the first regional wave, a minimum count of data points before promotion, and deployment windows that exclude nights, weekends and often Fridays ([Liguori](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)). Google kept release trains ("if you're late for the release train, it will leave without you"), launch checklists, Test Engineers and "a great deal of manual testing" where judgment is needed ([Software Engineering at Google, ch. 24](https://abseil.io/resources/swe-book/html/ch24.html); [Whittaker, 2011](https://testing.googleblog.com/2011/03/how-google-tests-software-part-five.html)). Facebook in 2013 still cut a weekly branch on Sunday for a Tuesday push, required every contributing engineer to be online during it, and kept a release-engineering team with the power to refuse and revert ([Feitelson et al., 2013](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/pcr/courses/csce431/fall19/reading/FacebookDevelopment.pdf)); its iOS app in 2016 kept five days of stabilization followed by a **three-day soak**, on employees and beta users, because a shipped binary cannot be recalled ([Rossi et al., FSE 2016](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Rossi-FSE-16.pdf)). Azure DevOps still deploys on a three-week sprint cadence, promotes through five tiers with about 24 hours of bake each, and had a lead engineer approve the first ring ([Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices); [Almeida, 2016](https://www.infoq.com/articles/devops-lessons-microsoft)). Netflix in 2013 still had a weekly release branch started by "someone on our delivery team" ([Netflix, 2013](https://netflixtechblog.com/deploying-the-netflix-api-79b6176cc3f0)). LinkedIn admitted in 2019, eight years after its tooling pause, that many teams "traditionally relied on a manual release process" ([LinkedIn, 2019](https://engineering.linkedin.com/blog/2019/learnings-from-the-journey-to-continuous-deployment)).

### What actually changed is five properties of the wait

Amazon's account makes the difference from a pre-production soak explicit. The wait runs in production on real customer traffic, starting with one box that serves at most about ten percent of a Region's requests. It is per stage, so exposure grows only as evidence accumulates. The exit criterion is alarm state plus a minimum number of data points (the example is at least 100 requests to an API), not elapsed time alone. Failure triggers automatic rollback, often "already in progress by the time the oncall engineer has been paged". And changes enter the pipeline continuously, "so we don't end up with large bundles of changes waiting" ([Liguori](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)). Netflix made the same substitution in its canary: a statistical comparison of a new cluster against a fresh baseline cluster under the same live traffic replaced a week of watching for breakage, and canaries came to "take hours instead of days" ([Kayenta, 2018](https://netflixtechblog.com/automated-canary-analysis-at-netflix-with-kayenta-3260bc7acc69); [Netflix, 2018](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249)). Batch size, not gate count, is the variable all of them changed: Amazon's unit of release became the single change, LinkedIn's rule became that "anything sitting in trunk must be releasable at any point in time" ([Wired, 2013](https://www.wired.com/2013/04/linkedin-software-revolution/), journalism quoting Kevin Scott), and Facebook's average deployed update was 92 lines, median 33 ([Savor et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)).

Rings and flags were often adopted after flags alone had failed in public. On the morning of the Visual Studio 2013 launch the Azure DevOps team switched on a batch of flags on a single-instance service; rolling them all back "didn't" restore service, "And no one is completely sure why", and Brian Harry's diagnosis was that "all customers are affected or none" ([Harry, 2013](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/a-rough-patch/)). The first remedy, weekend deployments, was "a mitigation" and unsustainable; ring 0 followed in early 2014, and later guidance reversed to deploying in working hours, early in the week, once blast radius was controllable ([Harry, 2014](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/visual-studio-online-update-feb-10/); [Microsoft Learn](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/devops/operate/safe-deployment-practices)). That reversal is the cleanest documented example of risk perception changing as a consequence of engineering, not exhortation.

## Managers moved the pager, the approval and the blame before they removed any gate

### Ownership and on-call went to the authors, completely or not at all

The founding statement is Werner Vogels in 2006: "You build it, you run it", justified as a quality feedback loop, with the concession in the same interview that Amazon had "no simple answers" on building a full-scale test replica ([ACM Queue, 2006](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=1142065)). Netflix's account is the most useful to a manager because it records a failed half-measure. A hybrid model in which developers could push but a release specialist remained "left gaps": developers "would often defer to the ops release specialist", and operations staff "found it hard to prioritize automation so that others didn't need to rely on them". Full ownership worked only with central tooling teams, bootcamps, an on-call rotation and teams "staffed appropriately with enough headroom"; "Without these, teams risk overload and burnout" ([Netflix, 2018](https://netflixtechblog.com/full-cycle-developers-at-netflix-a08c31f83249)). Facebook enforced presence, not perfection: an engineer whose change shipped while they were unreachable had it reverted and lost "push karma", a private score reported to their manager; the penalty was for absence when the change broke, not for the breakage ([Feitelson et al.](https://people.engr.tamu.edu/pcr/courses/csce431/fall19/reading/FacebookDevelopment.pdf); [Rossi interview, 2019](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/SEDFB14-Facebook-Release-Engineering.pdf)). Azure DevOps formalised designated responsible individuals in October 2013 and rotates two members of each ten-person team onto live-site duty (reported by [Bekker, 2017](https://rcpmag.com/blogs/scott-bekker/2017/11/15-microsoft-lessons-in-devops.aspx), relayed). Google shows the choice is not binary: it kept a specialist reliability function but capped its operational work at 50% and can hand the pager back to a development team whose service is too unreliable ([SRE, ch. 1](https://sre.google/sre-book/introduction/)).

### QA was redefined and moved off the release path; where it was deleted outright the evidence is thin

Google made testers scarce, moved them out of the product reporting line and turned them into tool-builders and a late "double-check"; James Whittaker's line is that "it's the product teams that own quality, not testers" ([Whittaker, 2011](https://testing.googleblog.com/2011/01/how-google-tests-software.html)). That culture was retrofitted from about 2005 by volunteers "without explicit executive support" and was finished by tooling in 2010, roughly five years later ([Bland, 2014](https://martinfowler.com/articles/testing-culture.html)). Atlassian renamed the function "quality assistance" and said it took "five years to get the Jira team to the level of quality mindset it has today", using a temporary developer-on-test stage as scaffolding ([Atlassian, 2014](https://www.atlassian.com/blog/archives/qa-webinar-deliver-quality-assurance-speed-recap$)). Azure DevOps merged development and test into one engineering organisation around late 2014 to 2015 and threw away tens of thousands of tests over 42 sprints; Harry lists what was surrendered ("clearly measurable and controllable investment in test, expertise and career growth in the testing discipline") and admits that eighteen months in "I started to agitate about when we were going to be 'done'" ([Harry, 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)). IMVU, at fifty deploys a day, kept QA engineers spending "at least half their time manually testing" inside teams, because "you can't catch all regressions or prevent all bugs with automated tests" ([Birchler, republished 2025](https://jamesbirchler.substack.com/p/imvus-approach-to-integrating-quality-assurance-with-continuous-deployment-for-imvu-engineering-blog)). Facebook's "no QA" had contractors and specialty testers who never blocked a release, about 100 contracted manual testers on mobile, and a release-engineering team that Savor and colleagues judge "valuable when reliable software is important" ([Rossi interview, 2019](https://softwareengineeringdaily.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/08/SEDFB14-Facebook-Release-Engineering.pdf); [Savor et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)). Yahoo's 2014-2015 removal of QA is the big-bang, no-exceptions pattern, supported only by two executives' assertions in a magazine interview, with no defect or headcount data ([IEEE Spectrum, 2015](https://spectrum.ieee.org/yahoos-engineers-move-to-coding-without-a-net)). No source read gives headcounts of testers or release managers redeployed, retrained or laid off at any firm.

### Approval moved to peer review, the pipeline and a visible record

At AWS "the code review is the last manual review and approval that a code change receives from an engineer", reviewers are asked whether the change can be rolled back safely, and senior scrutiny reappears only to go faster than the pipeline: shortening bake time needs Principal Engineer review ([Liguori](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)). The Financial Times replaced its board with peer-reviewed pull requests plus an API call that logs each release with commit and person ([Wells, 2019](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/microservices-financial-times/)). Etsy announced every push in chat with author and diff ([Kastner, 2010](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/quantum-of-deployment)). The reformist position is older than the abolitionist one: Jez Humble's 2010 post argues that ITIL's own pre-authorised "standard changes" permit a low-ceremony process and that "Face-to-face CAB meetings serve an important purpose" ([Humble, 2010](https://continuousdelivery.com/2010/11/continuous-delivery-and-itil-change-management)), and the 2019 State of DevOps report recasts the board as "process architect and information beacon" ([DORA 2019](https://dora.dev/research/2019/dora-report/2019-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf), p. 51). Flickr's operations team kept its go/no-go vote; John Allspaw's letter to Yahoo management says "A significant part of my job was working towards saying 'go', not 'no-go'", and insists that the dark launches and flags that made "go" safe "were concepts and tools created by Flickr Development, not Flickr Operations" ([Allspaw, 2012](https://www.kitchensoap.com/2012/01/05/convincing-management-that-cooperation-and-collaboration-was-worth-it/)).

Authority to undo was made cheaper than authority to proceed. Google's customer-reliability guidance is that "rollbacks are normal", that "the releasing team rolls back first and investigates the problem second", and that "No-one will ask 'why did you roll back this change?'" provided the rollback record describes the problem ([Hilton, 2017](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons), relayed by a fetch tool). Booking.com's rule is that "Everyone in the company must be able and feel empowered to stop harmful experiments of their own accord", backed by attribution of each experiment's effect in under a minute; it deliberately declined to automate the stop ([Kaufman et al., 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.08217)). Amazon, Monzo and Slack made the opposite choice and delegated the decision to the pipeline, wired to the same signals that page a human. Three authority models coexist in the sources and should not be blurred: the author rolls back their own change; anyone may stop a change they see hurting; and, once an incident is declared, one trained commander decides and others do not freelance ([SRE, ch. 14](https://sre.google/sre-book/managing-incidents/)).

### Incentives and blame were treated as information problems

The experimentation literature is blunt that the obstacle was power and reward. Kohavi and colleagues wrote in 2009 that "Most goals in software development organizations at Microsoft are around 'shipping' products, not about their impact on customers or key metrics", that some people saw experiments "as a risk to their power and/or prestige", and that after three years "it is unlikely that we have made a dent in many people's yearly performance goals" ([Kohavi, Crook, Longbotham, 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)). Amazon's adoption metric backfired in one respect: counting automated pipelines "could lead teams to automate their release process without providing for any testing", and the corrective was ownership of consequences, not a new gate ([Mansour](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/going-faster-with-continuous-delivery/)). Allspaw's argument for blameless review is aimed at managers: an engineer expecting punishment is "disincentivized to give the details necessary", and "Management becomes less aware and informed on how work is being performed day to day"; engineers "are not at all 'off the hook'" ([Allspaw, 2012](https://www.etsy.com/codeascraft/blameless-postmortems)). The reflex after a failure is the test of a management team. At OANDA, after a failure "that cost the company a significant amount of money within seconds", senior management first reached for more hierarchy, more approvals and "a full-blown, separate and traditional testing team"; the postmortem found that failing unit tests had flagged the bug and been ignored, and engineering persuaded management to put test output into the deploy decision instead. The same paper records a natural experiment: under executives inclined to traditional process, average deployments "dropped by 23%" with the engineering team largely unchanged ([Savor et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)). The 2019 State of DevOps report names the organisational version of that reflex: "Organizations often respond to problems with software releases by introducing additional process and more heavyweight approvals. Analysis suggests this approach will make things worse" ([DORA 2019](https://dora.dev/research/2019/dora-report/2019-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf), pp. 50-51). Google's error budget turns the argument into a contract: the business sets the reliability target, the shortfall from 100% is a budget development may spend on launches, and when it is spent releases halt; the chapter lists "How long do we wait, and how big is the canary?" among the disputes the budget is meant to settle ([SRE, ch. 3](https://sre.google/sre-book/embracing-risk/)). An availability ratio does not express a ledger error, so a finance version needs correctness and reconciliation indicators the SRE texts do not supply.

### Platform investment came first, was large, and took years

Tooling teams were about **5% of engineers at Facebook and 15% at OANDA**, and OANDA "spent 2 years perfecting continuous deployment for mission critical infrastructure" ([Savor et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)). LinkedIn's leadership halted feature development in late 2011 to rebuild tooling ([LinkedIn, 2015](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/architecture/brief-history-scaling-linkedin)); Wired, quoting Kevin Scott, puts the halt at two months. Monzo says of automated rollback that "migration and tooling work was >90% of the effort" ([Monzo, 2022](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/11/02/argo-rollouts-at-scale)). Amazon's adoption technique is the most transferable to a release-train firm: sceptical teams were told to "encode their current release process, manual steps and all, in a pipeline" and to switch on automation stage by stage as confidence grew ([Mansour](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/going-faster-with-continuous-delivery/)). LinkedIn's "3x3" rule shows the opposite device, a deliberate constraint: three hours from commit to internal users "is not enough time to conduct any manual testing steps", which "ensures we won't revert to using manual validation" ([LinkedIn, 2016](https://engineering.linkedin.com/blog/2016/02/3x3--speeding-up-mobile-releases)). Sequence matters. Slack built alerts with manual remediation first and automatic rollback second ([Slack, 2025](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/)); Microsoft's experimentation model introduces small initial allocation, auto-ramp and auto-shutdown before it delegates ramp authority to product teams ([Fabijan et al., ICSE 2017](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/2017-05-ICSE2017_EvolutionOfExP.pdf)); and at leading regulated firms exposure control matured late, with Monzo still "rolling out a project to bring automated rollbacks" in September 2022, years after it reached hundreds of deploys a day ([Patel, QCon Plus 2022](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/monzo-tools-deploy/)). Harry's judgment on six years of change at Azure DevOps is "If we'd tried to do it all at once. I'm sure we would have failed" ([Harry, 2017](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/testing-in-a-cloud-delivery-cadence/)). DORA's leadership finding adds the caution that sponsorship alone is insufficient: "leaders cannot achieve DevOps outcomes on their own" ([DORA 2017](https://dora.dev/research/2017/2017-state-of-devops-report.pdf)).

## Each alternative replaces one job of the testing period, and each has a documented limit

A testing period does several jobs at once: it finds functional regressions, exercises load and elapsed-time behaviour, lets humans accept the change, bundles approval, and provides a back-out point. ### Rollback is three different mechanisms, and only one is instant

"Rollback" means three things in the sources. Re-pointing traffic at a still-running old version (Netflix's red/black push, blue-green, a Lambda alias) takes seconds to minutes and needs no deployment. Re-deploying a retained immutable artifact (Amazon pipelines, Kubernetes ReplicaSets, Heroku slugs) takes one deployment cycle. Re-deploying an earlier source revision (Etsy in 2010, GitHub in 2015) is only as trustworthy as the build is repeatable. Measured durations are scarce: Wealthfront reported "about five seconds to complete across a cluster of 10 machines" in 2010 ([Fortunato](https://eng.wealthfront.com/?p=1273)); Salesforce Edge reports global rollback falling "from eight to twelve hours to ten minutes" after it began keeping the prior version warm ([Salesforce Engineering](https://engineering.salesforce.com/inside-salesforce-edge-automating-global-rollback-for-1-5-trillion-requests-in-10-minutes/)); Meta says a failed deployment of a complex service "can take hours to mitigate" and refuses to keep old versions warm on cost grounds ([Conveyor](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi23/presentation/grubic)). The audit value is older than the speed value: Humble and Farley justify build-once "For auditing purposes", with hashes checked at every stage, and tools record a rollback as a new, logged release pointing at old content.

Rollback is safe only one compatible step back. Amazon found that "the most common reason for not being able to roll back is a change of protocol", splits such changes into prepare and activate phases separated by a bake of "usually a few days", and tests upgrade, coexistence and downgrade in the pipeline ([Pokkunuri](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/ensuring-rollback-safety-during-deployments/)). Google advises that "If you haven't rolled back in a few weeks, you should do a rollback 'just because'" ([Hilton](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/gcp/reliable-releases-and-rollbacks-cre-life-lessons)). Flag-off is the lighter reversal, preferred at Facebook to reverting the binary ([Rossi, 2017](https://engineering.fb.com/2017/08/31/web/rapid-release-at-massive-scale/)), but it reverses one behaviour, not a release, and a flag that has been at 100% for months has an untested off state. Canaries and rollback authority are complements: the [SRE Workbook](https://sre.google/workbook/canarying-releases/) assumes "detection and rollback take about the same time" with or without a canary, so a ramp limits how many customers are hurt and rollback limits for how long.

### What a soak still buys

The defect classes a soak targets are real: leaks, pool exhaustion, slow degradation and time-triggered work. Amazon keeps bake time because impact is sometimes "slow burning"; Kayenta's fresh baseline is deliberately blind to long-running effects; the SRE Workbook requires a canary to span at least "a single work unit" for non-interactive systems. Roblox ran a new feature clean for a day and Slack ran two 25% stages clean before each failed at peak load. The honest residue of the testing period is therefore a deliberate hold at limited exposure that spans a load cycle and, for batch, a work unit. What the sources do not support is a uniform multi-day hold in a smaller environment for every change. Yuan and colleagues found that 98% of sampled catastrophic failures in distributed systems reproduced on three or fewer nodes and 77% by a unit test, which argues for forcing long-running states in targeted tests instead of waiting for them, and equally argues against the claim that pre-release testing is futile ([Yuan et al., OSDI 2014](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/conference/osdi14/osdi14-paper-yuan.pdf)).

## The new way is better on fidelity, batch size and recovery, and the proof is strongest where regulators measured

### Staging answers a different question from production

Practitioner accounts agree on the fidelity gaps: cluster size, configuration that differs because scale differs, data shape, traffic mix, monitoring and cross-team drift ([Sridharan, 2018](https://copyconstruct.medium.com/testing-in-production-the-safe-way-18ca102d0ef1); [Uber, 2020](https://www.uber.com/blog/multitenancy-microservice-architecture/); [DoorDash, 2022](https://doordash.engineering/2022/03/03/moving-e2e-testing-into-production-with-multi-tenancy-for-increased-speed-and-reliability/)). The academic outage study of 597 incidents at 32 services found upgrades the largest known root cause, at 16% of tagged causes, with upgrades that "had been tested" failing "when the updates were pushed to the full ecosystem" ([Gunawi et al., SoCC 2016](https://ucare.cs.uchicago.edu/pdf/socc16-cos.pdf)). Two regulator and vendor documents make the point concretely. Atlassian's April 2022 deletion script "was tested in Staging per our standard change management processes" and passed peer review; staging could not catch the fault because the wrong identifiers did not exist there; 775 customers lost access for up to 14 days; and the remedy was soft delete plus progressive rollout ([Atlassian PIR](https://www.atlassian.com/engineering/post-incident-review-april-2022-outage)). The FCA found that TSB dropped a non-functional test of its two-data-centre configuration, and that "Had this testing been conducted, it is likely that a problem in the configuration of certain components in the data centres ... would have been found" ([FCA Final Notice, para. 2.14](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf)). Stripe's July 2019 outage came from a database version that had passed QA, a phased rollout and about three months in production before a rare combination of stalled nodes exposed it ([Stripe RCA](https://stripe.com/rcas/2019-07-10)): elapsed time, in any environment, does not find a fault whose trigger does not occur. Lyft's experience is the measured counterweight: it kept shared staging, made it a paged first-class environment with a ten-minute bake, deleted most pre-merge integration tests, and reported "no measurable increase in the number of bugs reaching production" ([Lyft, part 4](https://eng.lyft.com/scaling-productivity-on-microservices-at-lyft-part-4-gating-deploys-with-automated-acceptance-4417e0ebc274)).

### Batching is a risk the soak does not remove

Larger and more diffuse changes fail more often in the classic telecom study of about 15,000 changes ([Mockus and Weiss, 2000](http://mockus.us/papers/bltj13.pdf)). Facebook found that code pushed on the day of the release cut "is of lower quality" ([Rossi et al., FSE 2016](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Rossi-FSE-16.pdf)), and Amazon warns that narrow deployment windows make changes "pile up". The finance-specific evidence is the OANDA comparison. OANDA, an FX platform, licensed the same code to large banks whose "policies did not allow continuous deployment"; at the banks, bugs deemed non-critical went unfixed "for many months", each release bundled "thousands of changes", which "increased anxiety" and led to much more testing, and failures were harder to isolate ([Savor et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)). That comparison is qualitative and written by insiders, but it describes the reader's situation exactly. The Financial Times supplies the cleanest before-and-after numbers found anywhere: roughly 16% of its 12 yearly releases failed, against fewer than 25 of about 2,500 releases in 2017, under 1%, by the speaker's own definition of failure ([Wells, 2019](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/microservices-financial-times/)).

### The regulator's own data and the survey record point the same way

The FCA's multi-firm review, published 5 February 2021 on calendar-2019 data from 23 firms, is the single most useful source for this audience. It found that "firms that deployed smaller, more frequent releases had higher change success rates than those with longer release cycles"; that nearly 68,000 major changes produced a failure rate of **3.8% against 1.6%** for all changes "despite the increased oversight"; that "CABs approved over 90% of the major changes they reviewed, and in some firms the CAB had not rejected a single change during 2019", which "raises questions over the effectiveness of CABs as an assurance mechanism"; and that change caused 17% of material incidents reported to it but 24% of firms' high-severity incidents ([FCA review](https://www.fca.org.uk/publications/multi-firm-reviews/implementing-technology-change)). The review is correlational, publishes no effect sizes for the frequency finding, does not recommend abolishing change boards, and contains its own caveat: firms that described more of their work as "DevOps" had "a slightly higher change failure rate", with no firm conclusion because of sample size.

The State of DevOps surveys agree in direction and are weaker in kind. The 2014 report found "external change approval boards had a big negative impact on throughput, with negligible impact on stability" ([DORA 2014](https://dora.dev/research/2014/2014-state-of-devops-report.pdf), p. 18); the 2019 report found respondents "2.6 times more likely to be low performers" where an external body approved changes, "no evidence" that formal approval lowers change failure, and no effect of industry on performance apart from retail, naming financial services as able to achieve high performance ([DORA 2019](https://dora.dev/research/2019/dora-report/2019-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf), pp. 19, 50). These are cross-sectional, self-reported, snowball-sampled surveys; reverse causation is live, since fragile organisations may both keep boards and perform poorly; the famous multipliers are arithmetic on answer-category midpoints; and financial services was 12% of roughly 1,000 respondents in 2019. The essay should write "associated with", not "causes".

### Company outcome data shows coexistence, and one large measured improvement

At Facebook from 2008 to 2014, critical production issues grew far more slowly than deployments: "The number of critical issues arising from deployments was almost constant regardless of the number of deployments"; the authors add "we do not claim that continuous deployment is necessarily a key contributor" ([Savor et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Savor-ICSE16.pdf)). Shortening Facebook's mobile cycle from four weeks to one "does not appear to affect the crash rate" ([Rossi et al.](https://www.eecg.toronto.edu/~stumm/Papers/Rossi-FSE-16.pdf)). Firefox's move to rapid release produced no significant increase in post-release bugs and median fix time fell from 16 days to 6, though crashes came earlier in a session and a smaller share of bugs was fixed ([Khomh et al., 2012](https://swat.polymtl.ca/~foutsekh/docs/Khomh-MSR-2012.pdf)). Slack reports that 73% of its customer-facing incidents were triggered by its own changes and that customer impact hours fell 90% from peak between February 2024 and January 2025, with the drop following the introduction of automatic rollbacks in April 2024; the programme had many parts and the baseline is the peak ([Slack, 2025](https://slack.engineering/deploy-safety/), relayed). Microsoft's Office study gives the only data on how long a ring needs: 35% of early-ring controlled rollouts moved a guardrail metric, 79% of those within the first three days and the rest by day seven ([Xia et al., 2019](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2020/06/Safe-Velocity-ICSE-SEI.pdf)). Experiment base rates explain why opinion-based acceptance is weak: at Microsoft "only about one-third" of well-designed experiments improved the metric they targeted ([Kohavi et al., 2009](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/ExP_DMCaseStudies.pdf)), and a re-implemented Amazon order pipeline believed equivalent lost 2% of revenue in its A/B test and "shipped after five iterations" ([Kohavi et al., 2013](https://exp-platform.com/Documents/2013%20controlledExperimentsAtScale.pdf)). Those rates concern whether a change helps, not whether pre-release testing finds defects.

### What "better overall" can and cannot claim

The firm claim is this: frequent, small, exposure-limited change is compatible with high stability at regulated money-moving firms; the regulator's own data associates it with higher change success; large batches and heavyweight external approval show no measured stability benefit; and a ramp tests the system that will actually serve clients. Three things are not established. No source measures incidents before and after a payments firm or bank left release trains. No source measures defects caught per day of soak, and Google could not measure how many outages its canary service prevents ([Davidovič, 2018](https://queue.acm.org/detail.cfm?id=3194655)). And speed without built-in quality is worse: DORA's 2017 low performers sped up and reported higher failure rates, Facebook's 2009-2010 productivity surge "coincided with a degradation in quality", and DORA's 2018 "misguided performers", who release rarely and test heavily, did achieve a better change-failure rate than low performers while reporting the longest time to restore service ([DORA 2018](https://dora.dev/research/2018/dora-report/2018-dora-accelerate-state-of-devops-report.pdf), p. 16). Caution buys something on failure rate and costs heavily on recovery. The case rests on the bundle of small batch, automated checks, bounded exposure and fast, rehearsed reversal, never on frequency alone.

## In payments and core banking the rules ask for evidence, and the ramp unit is the client

### No rule text read requires a soak, a train or multi-day promotion

The texts read in full or in verified extracts require the same evidence set and none of the audience's calendar. PCI DSS v4.0 requires "Documented change approval by authorized parties", testing whose scope "will vary according to the type of change", separation of pre-production from production, and "Procedures to address failures and return to a secure state"; the words soak, canary, pipeline and advisory board do not appear ([PCI DSS v4.0](https://www.pcisecuritystandards.org/documents/PCI-DSS-v4_0.pdf)). The FFIEC's 2024 examination booklet says that "in a DevOps environment, the same number and type of changes can be made daily or even hourly", describes canary release as sending "a limited amount of traffic" to a new version, treats pilots and phased implementation as legitimate, lists change boards as "Examples" of advisory groups, and says routine modifications, "Sometimes referred to as standard changes", "are often implemented with automation" ([FFIEC DA&M, 2024](https://business.cch.com/BFLD/FFIEC-IT-DevelopmentAcquisitionMaintenance-Hankbook-08292024.pdf)). The EBA's 2019 guidelines require changes to be "recorded, tested, assessed, approved, implemented and verified in a controlled manner" and, after banks objected that the draft implied waterfall, state that they "intend to be technology and methodology agnostic" ([EBA/GL/2019/04](https://www.eba.europa.eu/sites/default/files/document_library/Publications/Guidelines/2020/GLs%20on%20ICT%20and%20security%20risk%20management/872936/Final%20draft%20Guidelines%20on%20ICT%20and%20security%20risk%20management.pdf)). EU DORA repeats that formula "based on a risk assessment approach" ([Regulation 2022/2554, Art. 9(4)(e)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32022R2554)). The PCAOB's internal-control standard prescribes no change procedure at all ([AS 2201](https://pcaobus.org/oversight/standards/auditing-standards/details/AS2201)), and the PRA's operational-resilience statement regulates outcomes, adding only that "firms that implement changes to their operations more frequently should undertake more frequent scenario testing" ([SS1/21, 6.6](https://www.bankofengland.co.uk/-/media/boe/files/prudential-regulation/supervisory-statement/2021/ss121-march-22.pdf)).

The texts do bite, and the essay should say where. MAS expects that "A change advisory board ... should be formed to approve and prioritise the changes", that "Test results should be accepted and signed off before the changes are deployed", that quality assurance be "performed by an independent quality assurance function", and that "no single individual has the ability to develop, compile and move software codes from one environment to another" ([MAS TRM 2021](https://www.mas.gov.sg/-/media/MAS/Regulations-and-Financial-Stability/Regulatory-and-Supervisory-Framework/Risk-Management/TRM-Guidelines-18-January-2021.pdf), 7.5.3, 7.5.4, 5.8.2, 7.6.1). The FFIEC booklet says "developers should not test and validate systems and components that they developed", gives as an example restricting production repositories "only to release management personnel", prefers deployment "during off-peak hours", and warns that automation with rapid release "may result in perpetuation of errors". PCI DSS 6.2.3.1 names three roles when code review is manual: author, reviewer, and a release-control approver who is neither. The DORA technical standards require the approving function to be independent of those requesting and implementing, and permit testing in production only as an identified, reasoned, time-limited and approved exception ([Delegated Regulation 2024/1774](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32024R1774), Arts. 17(1)(b) and 8(2)(b), read through a fetch tool that paraphrased them). The PCAOB standard makes frequent change to code containing automated financial controls more expensive to audit, because benchmarking favours applications with "few changes from period to period". No text read says whether an automated gate may substitute for a human approver, and none mentions feature flags.

### Firms passed audit by relocating approval, never by removing it

The control mapping recurs across Monzo, Starling, Capital One, Etsy's PCI environment and Google. Authorisation is a reviewer other than the author. Segregation is the absence of human write access to production, with the pipeline as the release-management actor. Evidence is an immutable log linking commit, review, test result and deploy. Exceptions are break-glass access with loud alerts and after-the-fact review. Monzo's "change management policy dictates that we require at least one other person to review a change", enforced by the pipeline, with append-only audit logs ([Monzo, 2022](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/03/31/how-we-secure-monzos-banking-platform)). Capital One's governance director, a former auditor, wrote that "The biggest hurdle is often that one person cannot do it all and maintain appropriate segregation of duties", recommended restricting production access for everyone and allowing change only through a "cleanroom" pipeline, and added that "Risk cannot be mitigated with typical 'CI/CD' automation alone" ([Brady, 2018](https://www.capitalone.com/tech/software-engineering/governance-in-a-devops-environment/)); its pipeline's sixteen gates include an automated change order, automated rollback and a feature toggle ([Pal, 2018](https://www.capitalone.com/tech/software-engineering/focusing-on-the-devops-pipeline/)). Etsy built "a whole separate Etsy, essentially" for cardholder data, with distinct pusher roles and a ticket for every change, and could still take a normal change to production in under an hour ([Humble reporting Rembetsy, 2012](https://continuousdelivery.com/2012/07/pci-dss-and-continuous-deployment-at-etsy)); Etsy's own slides say payment processing was continuously deployed only as far "as is safe & legal" ([Snyder, 2013](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/ross-synder-etsy-sxsw-lean-startup-2013/17023596), tool-extracted). The DORA 2019 report gives the generic recipe: peer approval at code review recorded as authenticated events in version control satisfies the intent of segregation of duties.

Starling is the best intermediate model for a twice-weekly-train firm, because it kept human gates and released daily. In 2017 it described "continual deployment to non-prod, sign-off into prod" and 134 software releases in its first 221 days of production; in 2018, "code released to production up to 5 times a day" with a "take ownership" ceremony in which "all engineers explicitly bless their commits in slack", and a release-in-progress signal of which "our auditors loved this one" ([Starling, QCon 2017 slides](https://qconlondon.com/london-2017/system/files/presentation-slides/qcon_0.pdf); [QCon 2018 slides](https://archive.qconlondon.com/system/files/presentation-slides/qcon_2018.pptx.pdf)). In 2019 its engineer said "we use Slack for people to gain permission in a four-eyes manner to initiate a deployment to production" ([QCon London panel](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/panel-release-risk)), and in 2025 the bank described three approvals before each of several daily releases: commit, technical, and a business approval that "is here to manage our overall risk as a regulated bank", all recorded in "our immutable audit of what software we release to production" ([Starling, 2025](https://technology.starlingbank.com/blog/how-we-made-everyone-a-release-engineer)). LMAX released fortnightly on Saturdays behind more than 10,000 automated tests, and IG deployed one to three times a week with a manual stop ([Bird, 2016](https://www.oreilly.com/content/introduction-to-devops-for-the-financial-industry/); [Genn, 2015](https://www.slideshare.net/DavidGenn/continuous-delivery-krakow-jdd-2015)). The decisive gap is that **no firm has published the outcome of a regulator examination or external audit of its change process**; the closest are a panel remark that "Sometimes the regulator's impressed" and Capital One's advice that auditors and regulators "must be brought into the conversation". Acceptance is implied by continued operation under supervision, not documented.

Supervisors' priors run toward caution. The ECB wrote in 2023 that "IT changes and software issues were again identified as the root cause behind critical services downtime" ([ECB, 2023](https://www.bankingsupervision.europa.eu/press/publications/newsletter/2023/html/ssm.nl231115.en.html)). After repeated outages MAS directed DBS in November 2023 to "suspend all changes to the bank's IT systems" for six months, apart from security, regulatory and risk changes ([MAS, 2023](https://www.mas.gov.sg/news/media-releases/2023/mas-imposes-six-month-pause-on-dbs)): a supervisor's remedy for change failure was less change. The argument that lands with a supervisor is failure rate and blast radius per change, with evidence; it is never speed.

### Flags and rollbacks need a named place in the change policy

A production flag flip is plainly a change under the scope clauses (DORA covers "security parameters"; FFIEC covers "configuration settings"), so the defensible position is to give flag changes their own logged, approved, pre-authorised change type. A workable classification, which is this report's synthesis and not accepted practice stated by any source, is that the code introducing a flag goes through reviewed deployment, the ramp plan with its stages and abort criteria is approved once as a standard change model, each ramp step is a logged execution of that model, and kill-switch use is an emergency change with after-the-fact review. The same reasoning applies to rollback. MAS places the rollback plan inside the original change and requires emergency approvers to be identified in advance; the EBA requires only "adequate safeguards" for emergencies; none of the texts says executing a rollback needs fresh approval, and none says it does not. Trading regulation already contains the asymmetry the essay argues for. MiFID II's RTS 6 requires that a firm "be able to cancel immediately, as an emergency measure, any or all of its unexecuted orders", requires predefined limits on a newly deployed algorithm's instruments, order values and positions, and requires designated authorisation to deploy ([Delegated Regulation 2017/589](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32017R0589), Arts. 5, 8, 12; relayed): turning exposure up is controlled, turning it off must be immediate. No payments regulation imposing an equivalent kill capability was found, and no bank or payments firm has published how it classifies flag flips or rollbacks under change control.

### Money-moving changes are ramped by making the decision reversible, not the money

Practitioners do not claim money movement is reversible. Kent Beck's own account of Facebook's reversibility culture carves it out: "Decisions affecting people's privacy, trust, and money; and decisions affecting the Facebook brand aren't reversible" and "need to follow completely different workflows" ([Beck, 2015](https://newsletter.kentbeck.com/p/taming-complexity-with-reversibility)); Allspaw's recovery-first argument is scoped to "most types of F" and excludes failures that "shouldn't ever happen" ([Allspaw, 2010](https://www.kitchensoap.com/2010/11/07/mttr-mtbf-for-most-types-of-f/)). The payments firms' answer is to front-load that different workflow into design. Three kinds of reversibility need separating. The routing decision (a flag, a percentage, a phase) is reversible in seconds to minutes. Internal ledger state is correctable by compensating entries with the error preserved: Square's ledger is "effectively append-only and immutable once stored", corrected by "a new entry that corrects the previous one" ([Square, 2019](https://developer.squareup.com/blog/books-an-immutable-double-entry-accounting-database-service/)). Externally settled money is not reversible by the firm alone, so the techniques aim to keep that exposure small.

The documented techniques are specific. Idempotency bounds retries: Starling's rule is "every action happens at least once, and at most once", with "Give EVERYTHING a UUID" ([QCon 2018 slides](https://archive.qconlondon.com/system/files/presentation-slides/qcon_2018.pptx.pdf)). Shadow validation avoids repeating the side effect: Uber could not "call a PSP's endpoint twice", so it cached the legacy system's outbound requests and responses, ran the new service on delayed copies of production requests, compared the request it would have sent, and found "dozens of argument mismatches" before any user exposure ([Uber, 2019](https://www.uber.com/blog/migrating-functionality-between-production-systems)). Old-versus-new comparison finds what tests miss: Square compared the latest 1,000 payments on both paths, which "actually caught a lot of subtle bugs", and ran reads on the new path for about six weeks before moving writes ([Square, 2016](https://developer.squareup.com/blog/breaking-up-with-your-monorail/)); Stripe compared point-in-time snapshots before each sub-two-second cutover and kept replication bidirectional ([Stripe, 2024](https://stripe.dev/blog/how-stripes-document-databases-supported-99.999-uptime-with-zero-downtime-data-migrations)). Exposure is ramped by a nameable unit: Uber moved authorisation logic to employees, then real users at 0.25%, 1%, 5% and upward, one payment method at a time, and moved its ledger at 1%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 50% and 100% per country, starting with a small one ([Uber, 2020](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/money-scale-strong-data/)); Nubank rolled its card authoriser to "larger and larger sets of cards" ([Nubank, 2017](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/nubank-architecture/)). Invariants act as a continuous production test: Monzo's coherence checks can catch a bug affecting 0.1% of payments that raises no error, and daily reconciliation compares ledger with settlement ([Monzo, 2022](https://monzo.com/blog/2022/02/08/processing-payments-safely-at-scale)); Stripe verifies 99.99% of dollar volume within four days ([Stripe, 2024](https://stripe.dev/blog/ledger-stripe-system-for-tracking-and-validating-money-movement)).

These accounts carry three cautions. First, they are slow where correctness is at stake. Uber's ledger-store migration set a bar of "at least 99.99% complete and correct as measured by shadow validation" and did the arithmetic: six nines needs about 100 million comparisons, a little over a day at 1,000 records per second, seven nines about twelve days, and live shadowing missed cold data so an offline job compared 760 billion records; the migration took about two years ([Uber, 2024](https://www.uber.com/blog/migrating-from-dynamodb-to-ledgerstore/)). A core-banking system with lower request rates would need longer for the same confidence. Shadowing replaces calendar-driven soak with evidence-driven soak; it does not make time disappear. Second, detection latency sets the stage length: where reconciliation is daily and verification takes days, a ramp stage for a settlement-affecting change plausibly has to span at least one reconciliation cycle, which is this report's inference and is stated by no source. Third, rollback is itself a hazard: Stripe's second outage on 10 July 2019 occurred because "our revert to a known stable version interacted poorly with a recently-introduced configuration change" ([Stripe RCA](https://stripe.com/rcas/2019-07-10)), and an engineer on the 2019 fintech panel said that with immutable event logs "rolling back just never becomes an option". No first-person account was found of amount caps or low-value-first canaries as a rollout technique, of payment-specific kill switches in detail, or of mismatch rates from a named payments firm's shadow comparison.

### Period-boundary batch is the weakest-evidenced area and the best surviving argument for a hold

No first-person account was found from any continuously deploying bank or payments firm of how changes to end-of-day, interest accrual, statement, settlement-file or month-end jobs are validated before the boundary arrives. What exists is indirect: Starling treats interest accrual and ledger postings as retried idempotent commands that are "merely delayed" in an outage; Monzo's coherence rules include time-based expectations; Stripe's ledger can be replayed. A three-day soak does not cover the cases its defenders cite either, since month-end, quarter-end and leap days fall outside most three-day windows. The replacement recipe (run the new job in shadow against production data through at least one real boundary and diff its output against the incumbent; replay prior periods from an immutable ledger; clock-controlled tests; ramp by account cohort so the first real period-end touches few accounts) is a reasoned proposal assembled from adjacent evidence, and the essay should label it as such. Where a job is one-shot with external effect, such as a settlement file submitted at a cut-off, the evidence supports keeping a deliberate hold: a shadow cycle or rehearsal before first live use, scoped to that job.

### Shared infrastructure still runs as dated trains, and firms ramp around it

Release trains persist where the unit of change is a multi-party contract. Visa's network releases fall in April and October with dated mandates, as Braintree's merchant guide puts it: "Many of these changes occur twice per year, in April and October" ([PayPal Braintree release guide](https://developer.paypal.com/braintree/articles/risk-and-security/compliance/network-updates/2024/f-2024-all)). SWIFT's standards release is annual. The PSD2 technical standards require changes to an access interface's specification to be published "not less than 3 months before the change is implemented" ([Delegated Regulation 2018/389, Art. 30(4)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32018R0389)). The ECB moved its T2 go-live by four months in 2022 citing "the need to allow users more time to complete their testing in a stable environment" ([ECB, 2022](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/press/pr/date/2022/html/ecb.pr221020~432ea01228.en.html)). The Bank of England staged its RTGS renewal into "transition states" that could each stand alone, but "the old and new systems could not operate simultaneously: the new system had to replace the old one over a launch weekend (a 'big bang' approach)"; it used dress rehearsals, attestations and three months of hypercare, and launched about 18 months late at £431 million, 15% over budget ([NAO, 2025](https://www.nao.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/the-bank-of-englands-real-time-gross-settlement-system-renewal-programme.pdf)). The boundary for the essay is clean: external interface changes are calendar-bound by rule or by counterparty; behaviour behind a stable interface is not. Firms ship support for the dated change early, dark, and activate on the mandated date. Freezes also survive where incident cost peaks: Shopify, at Black Friday 2020, required director approval for merchant-facing changes and lengthened canaries, and admitted "we still did take a hit in developer productivity" ([Shopify, 2020](https://shopify.engineering/organizing-2000-developers-bfcm-remotely)).

### Experiments on financial clients are three different things with three different duties

The sources support separating three practices that share machinery. A shadow or parallel run computes the new result on live inputs while the old result is used, so no customer is exposed; finance wrote this into capital regulation, where the Basel II draft rule defines "a satisfactory parallel run" as "at least four consecutive calendar quarters" of computing old and new capital ratios side by side ([Federal Reserve, 2006 draft](https://www.federalreserve.gov/GeneralInfo/Basel2/DraftNPR/NPR/section_3.htm)). A safety ramp intends the same outcome for every customer and limits exposure in case of regression. A product experiment deliberately gives customers different acceptable variants to learn which serves them better. Calling a ramp an "experiment" in front of risk and compliance invites objections that belong only to the third category; calling a product experiment a "rollout" hides that customers are being treated differently on purpose.

The finance-native ramp unit is the client, not an anonymous percentage. Stripe pins each account to an API version on first request and lets the account choose when to upgrade ([Stripe, 2017](https://stripe.com/blog/api-versioning)); Adyen exposes experiments as a setting a merchant user with a named role turns on, and describes testing features through "'early adopter' merchants" ([Adyen docs](https://docs.adyen.com/uplift/uplift-experiments); [Adyen, 2020](https://www.adyen.com/knowledge-hub/the-adyen-way-of-engineering-expose-work-early)); Stripe's payment-method A/B tool lets the merchant choose a split from 0.01% to 99.99% ([Stripe, 2024](https://stripe.com/blog/how-we-built-it-payment-method-a-b-testing)); Nubank moved one shard of about 20 customers first ([CNCF, 2020](https://www.cncf.io/case-studies/nubank/)). Square's lesson is that cohorts should be stable per merchant, because random per-request percentages hid a regression that hit one six-month-old client version ([Square, 2016](https://developer.squareup.com/blog/breaking-up-with-your-monorail/)). Monzo publishes the fullest ladder: staff, opt-in Labs, randomised experiment with a control group, staged rollout with a "clear plan to roll back if necessary", then a silent period; it ran an experiment on a credit product's payment dates "to make sure it didn't lead to bad outcomes", and ran 43 experiments in a year on support flows with fixed guardrail metrics ([Monzo, 2023](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/08/03/how-we-launch-new-products-at-monzo); [Monzo, 2023](https://monzo.com/blog/2023/03/28/what-we-learned-from-43-experiments-in-12-months)). Published experiments at financial firms are overwhelmingly on interfaces, support, communications and payment-optimisation settings. No first-person account was found of randomised experiments on price, credit decisions, order routing or ledger behaviour, and no firm publishes guardrail thresholds or a list of what is never tested.

UK conduct regulation endorses experiments on real customers more plainly than most engineers expect. The FCA's Consumer Duty guidance says "customers' level of understanding could be tested directly by conducting randomised controlled trials or A/B tests", names A/B tests as legitimate evidence in fair-value assessments, and imposes a symmetry: firms that test communications "to maximise sales ... should use testing capabilities of an equivalent standard" for consumer understanding; different outcomes for different groups must be justified ([FCA FG22/5](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/finalised-guidance/fg22-5.pdf), paras. 8.41, 8.44, 6.36). The FCA has itself randomised letters to customers of regulated firms since 2013 ([FCA Occasional Paper 1](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/occasional-papers/occasional-paper-1.pdf)). The other edge is American: the FTC used Credit Karma's own A/B results as evidence that it knew its "pre-approved" wording misled, and ordered it to preserve records of research "including A/B testing" ([FTC, 2022](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2022/09/ftc-takes-action-stop-credit-karma-tricking-consumers-allegedly-false-pre-approved-credit-offers)). Experiment logs are discoverable, and a result showing a misleading variant converts better is evidence of knowledge. Delayed outcomes set durations here too: Adyen recommends "letting an experiment run for at least one month" for risk settings so that fraud and chargeback data mature ([Adyen docs](https://docs.adyen.com/risk-management/run-ab-test-experiments)). A canary catches crashes and declines in minutes; it does not catch a fraud regression.

## The failures indict unramped change classes, dormant paths and untested undo, not the ramp

### Knight Capital was a staged deployment with a single activation and a rollback that made it worse

The SEC order establishes that Knight's new code "repurposed a flag that was formerly used to activate the Power Peg code", unused since 2003 and left "present and callable"; that deployment began on 27 July 2012 "in stages by placing it on a limited number of servers in SMARS on successive days"; that a technician "did not copy the new code to one of the eight SMARS computer servers"; that "Knight did not have a second technician review this deployment" and "had no written procedures that required such a review"; that 97 automated emails reading "Power Peg disabled" went unread before the open; and that the result was 4 million executions in 154 stocks in about 45 minutes and a loss of more than $460 million ([SEC Release 34-70694](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-70694.pdf), paras. 13-19). Five days on production servers observed nothing because nothing exercised the path until the product launched: staged deployment of dormant code is not a ramp, and a soak would have seen the same nothing. The response compounded it: "Knight uninstalled the new RLP code from the seven servers where it had been deployed correctly. This action worsened the problem" (para. 27). The SEC's stated preventives are "a written procedure requiring a simple double-check of the deployment", integrating the reject messages into monitoring, and clear guidance on "when to disconnect a malfunctioning system from the market" (paras. 41-42). Knight therefore argues for uniform verified deployment, flag hygiene, automated guardrails on order flow and position, and a blunt high-level stop used first; it does not argue against four-eyes review, which the SEC faulted Knight for lacking.

### The banking and exchange record faults representativeness, one-shot events and the undo path

At RBS in June 2012 a batch-scheduler upgrade was backed out after anomalies, and the back-out caused the incident: "Technology Services had only tested the consequences of backing out Version 2 to Version 1", not the patched version actually installed, and "This was the underlying cause of the IT Incident"; at least 6.5 million customers were affected and the FCA fined the group £42 million ([FCA Final Notice, 2014](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/rbs-natwest-ulster-final-notice.pdf)). TSB chose "a predominantly single event data migration" for about 5.2 million customers in April 2018, compressed testing so that it "only concluded the day before the decision to migrate was taken", and could not roll back; it received 225,492 complaints, paid £32.7 million in redress, did not return to normal until 10 December 2018, and was fined £48.65 million by the FCA and PRA together ([FCA Final Notice, 2022](https://www.fca.org.uk/publication/final-notices/tsb-bank-plc-2022.pdf); [FCA press release](https://www.fca.org.uk/news/press-releases/tsb-fined-48m-operational-resilience-failings)). Nasdaq tested its IPO cross in production with a test symbol but capped the test at 40,000 orders; over 496,000 arrived for Facebook's listing, and the fix was improvised by executives who did not know the cause ([SEC Release 34-69655](https://www.sec.gov/litigation/admin/2013/34-69655.pdf)). In three of the finance cases the proximate cause is inconsistency between components during or after a change (one server of eight; two scheduler versions; gateways against a new release at NYSE in 2015), which is exactly the mixed-version state progressive delivery creates on purpose and must therefore test. Every regulator document locates root cause in governance, and none blames release cadence. The regulators' remedy language is "test more and govern better", not "test in production"; the essay should not put the second phrase in a regulator's mouth.

### Vendors' own postmortems sort into four patterns

The first pattern is a fast global path that bypassed the staged pipeline. CrowdStrike's sensor code had staged rollout but its content updates did not; its root-cause analysis concludes "Each Template Instance should be deployed in a staged rollout" with "additional bake-in time" ([CrowdStrike RCA, 2024](https://www.crowdstrike.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Channel-File-291-Incident-Root-Cause-Analysis-08.06.2024.pdf)). Cloudflare's 2019 outage came through a rule channel that "by design" skipped its staged process ([Cloudflare, 2019](https://blog.cloudflare.com/details-of-the-cloudflare-outage-on-july-2-2019/)); six years later a second global configuration path produced two outages in three weeks, one of them triggered by a kill switch applied through a system that "propagates changes within seconds to the entire fleet" ([Cloudflare, December 2025](https://blog.cloudflare.com/5-december-2025-outage/)). The second pattern is a dormant path that neither rollout nor soak exercised: Google Cloud's June 2025 outage came from code that had rolled out region by region two weeks earlier but "did not have appropriate error handling nor was it feature flag protected", activated everywhere by a policy write; Google's remedy is that "We will enforce all changes to critical binaries to be feature flag protected and disabled by default" ([Google Cloud incident report](https://status.cloud.google.com/incidents/ow5i3PPK96RduMcb1SsW)). The third is a change that passed its ramp and failed later or under load: Roblox ran a feature "well ... for a day before the incident started" and was down 73 hours ([Roblox, 2022](https://about.roblox.com/newsroom/2022/01/roblox-return-to-service-10-28-10-31-2021/)); Slack's third 25% step met peak load after two clean ones ([Slack, 2022](https://slack.engineering/slacks-incident-on-2-22-22/)); Facebook's small config canary missed a load problem, and an engineer overrode a correct canary rejection with "It must be a false positive of the canary tool!" ([Tang et al., 2015](https://sigops.org/s/conferences/sosp/2015/current/2015-Monterey/printable/008-tang.pdf)). The fourth is the safety machinery itself: Azure AD's safe-deployment system had a latent defect that sent a change to all rings at once and corrupted the metadata needed for automated rollback (Microsoft's analysis as quoted by [The Register, 2020](https://www.theregister.com/2020/10/02/microsoft_azure_bug/)), and Azure DevOps needed 45 minutes "to figure out how to turn it off" when a flag guarded the fault ([Harry, 2014](https://devblogs.microsoft.com/bharry/outage-on-monday-nov-24)).

The lessons convert into preconditions. Stage and bake configuration, flags, reference data and operator scripts like code; Amazon already sends "API rate limit increases and feature flags" through their own pipeline with automatic rollback ([Liguori](https://aws.amazon.com/builders-library/automating-safe-hands-off-deployments/)). Protect dormant paths with a default-off flag and exercise them deliberately. Hold at least one stage through a peak-load cycle. Treat internally generated configuration as untrusted input. Break dependencies between the thing changing and the tools needed to see and reverse it. Test the kill switch and the rollback routinely, on the real tooling, by the people who will use them. Make stopping easy and resuming hard: Google's own case study records a halted release restarted too early, where "it would have been better to postpone the rollout until the root cause was fully determined" ([SRE Workbook, ch. 9](https://sre.google/workbook/incident-response/)). After serious incidents the leaders added bake time: Azure Front Door slowed configuration propagation from five to ten minutes to about 45 ([Azure Networking Blog, 2025](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/azurenetworkingblog/-/4479416)). No vendor postmortem was found attributing an outage to having ramped instead of soaking in pre-production; the evidence concerns gaps in ramp coverage, and there is no controlled comparison either way.

## Conclusion

The essay's claim survives its evidence in a narrower and stronger form than "replace soak with flags". The testing period bundles four things: a place to wait, a batch, an approval and a way back. The record shows each can be had separately and better. The wait belongs in production at bounded exposure, because that is the only place the answer is about the real system. The batch should be one change, because the regulator's own data and the OANDA banks show that bundling is where risk and anxiety accumulate. The approval belongs with a qualified second person and a machine-written record, because boards that approve over 90% of what they see are not assurance. The way back should be a rehearsed, pre-authorised action on a hash that has already run, because the banking record's worst rollbacks were improvised. What remains of the old practice is legitimate and specific: a hold that spans a load cycle or a reconciliation cycle, a shadow run through a real period boundary for batch, rehearsal for cutovers that counterparties must make together, and dated trains for scheme and network interfaces.

For engineering managers at payments and core-banking firms, the implication is that the work is theirs and is mostly not about release mechanics. The sequence the sources support is to measure where the three days go, give one team the whole life cycle including the pager, encode the existing process in a pipeline and automate it stage by stage, bring audit and risk in as designers of the standard-change model, build comparison and reconciliation before the first ramp, and make rollback boring before making it automatic. The essay can be firm that the rules do not require the calendar, that the regulator's data favours small frequent change, and that the leaders kept more caution than their legend suggests. It should be silent on measured safety gains at banks, on what examiners concluded, and on month-end, because nobody has published those, and it should say that the firms which get there first will be the ones able to.
